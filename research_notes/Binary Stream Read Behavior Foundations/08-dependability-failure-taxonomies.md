## 2. Extracted findings (with verbatim quotes and locators)

### S1 Avizienis et al. 2004 (TR 2004-47), the fault-error-failure chain and service failure modes
- Failure definition (§2.2, p.4): "A service failure, often abbreviated here to failure, is an event that occurs when the delivered service deviates from correct service." The definition is relative to the **system function**, not only to the specification: "A service fails either because it does not comply with the functional specification, or because this specification did not adequately describe the system function."
- Error and fault (§2.2, p.4): "the definition of an error is: the part of the total state of the system that may lead to its subsequent service failure. It is important to note that many errors do not reach the system's external state and cause a failure. A fault is active when it causes an error, otherwise it is dormant." "The adjudged or hypothesized cause of an error is called a fault."
- Four viewpoints on failure modes (§3.3.1, p.13): "The service failure modes characterize incorrect service according to four viewpoints: a) the failure domain, b) the detectability of failures, c) the consistency of failures, and d) the consequences of failures on the environment."
  - Domain: "content failures: the content of the information delivered at the service interface (i.e., the service content) deviates from implementing the system function"; "timing failures: the time of arrival or the duration of the information delivered at the service interface ... deviates"; "a timing failure may be early or late"; when both content and timing are wrong, there is "halt failure ... (the external state becomes constant ...); a special case of halt is silent failure, or simply silence, when no service at all is delivered at the service interface" or "erratic failures otherwise, i.e., when a service is delivered (not halted), but is erratic (e.g., babbling)." Fig. 3.6 labels the late-timing case "Performance Failure".
  - Detectability: "When the losses are detected and signaled by a warning signal, then signaled failures occur. Otherwise, they are unsignaled failures. The detecting mechanisms themselves have two failure modes: a) signaling a loss of function when no failure has actually occurred, that is a false alarm, b) not signaling a function loss, that is an unsignaled failure."
  - Consistency (only "when a system has two or more users"): "consistent failures: the incorrect service is perceived identically by all system users"; "inconsistent failures: some or all system users perceive differently incorrect service (some users may actually perceive correct service); inconsistent failures are usually called, after [Lamport et al. 1982], Byzantine failures."
  - Consequences: from "minor failures" to "catastrophic failures". Severity criteria include "for integrity, the extent of the corruption of data and the ability to recover from these corruptions."
- Fail-controlled systems (§3.3.1, p.14): "A system whose failures are to an acceptable extent halting failures only, is a fail-halt (or fail-stop) system; the situations of stuck service and of silence lead respectively to fail-passive systems and fail-silent systems [Powell et al. 1988]." The phrase "to an acceptable extent" makes these probabilistic design targets, not absolute guarantees.
- Failure is subjective (§3.3.1, p.13): "So failures can be subjective and disputable, i.e., may require judgment to identify and characterize."
- Partial failure / degraded mode (§3.2/3.3 boundary, p.12): "the failure of one or more of the services ... may leave the system in a degraded mode that still offers a subset of needed services ... e.g., slow service, limited service, emergency service ... Here we say that the system has suffered a partial failure of its functionality or performance."
- Errors (§3.4, p.16): "An error is detected if its presence is indicated by an error message or error signal. Errors that are present but not detected are latent errors." Whether an error becomes a failure depends on redundancy and on behavior: "the part of the state that contains an error may never be needed for service, or an error may be eliminated (e.g., when overwritten) before it leads to a failure." Error-control-code classification: "content errors are further classified according to the damage pattern: single, double, triple, byte, burst, erasure, arithmetic, track, etc., errors."
- Recursion across components (§3.5, p.17): "the ensuing service failure of A appears as an external fault to B". "The failure of a component causes a permanent or transient fault in the system that contains the component."
- Persistence and reproducibility (§3.5, pp.17-18): "faults whose activation is reproducible are called solid, or hard, faults, whereas faults whose activation is not systematically reproducible are elusive, or soft, faults." "The similarity of the manifestation of elusive development faults and of transient physical faults leads to both classes being grouped together as intermittent faults. Errors produced by intermittent faults are usually termed soft errors."
- Coverage (§5.2.2, p.25): there is "fault assumption coverage, that can be in turn due to either i) failed component(s) not behaving as assumed, that is a lack of failure mode coverage, or ii) the occurrence of common-mode failures when independent ones are assumed, that is a lack of failure independence coverage." Also: "conservative fault assumptions (e.g., Byzantine faults) will result in a higher failure mode coverage, at the expense of necessitating an increase in the redundancy ... [Powell 1992]."
- **Read mapping [inference]:** a read is a service item delivered at a service interface. A wrong byte returned is a content failure. A slow return is a late-timing (performance) failure. No return at all is a halt or silent failure. An error code (e.g. EIO) is a signaled failure. Undetected corruption is an unsignaled content failure. Different replicas or readers seeing different values is an inconsistent (Byzantine) failure. A latent sector error is, in S1 terms, a dormant fault or latent error that a read activates.

### S2 Lamport, Shostak, Pease 1982, Byzantine failure and reading inputs
- Abstract: "Reliable computer systems must handle malfunctioning components that give conflicting information to different parts of the system." "using only oral messages, this problem is solvable if and only if more than two-thirds of the generals are loyal"; "With unforgeable written messages, the problem is solvable for any number of generals and possible traitors."
- §2: "no solution with fewer than 3m + 1 generals can cope with m traitors". This holds under the oral-message assumption: "An oral message is one whose contents are completely under the control of the sender, so a traitorous sender can transmit any possible message."
- §6 "Reliable Systems" (p.397) is directly about reading a value: "different processors can get different values even from a nonfaulty input unit if they read the value while it is changing. For example, if two processors read a clock while it is advancing, then one may get the old time and the other the new time. This can only be prevented by synchronizing the reads with the advancing of the clock." Also: "a faulty input unit could send a marginal signal along the wire--a signal that can be interpreted by some processors as a 0 and by others as a 1. There is no way to guarantee that different processors will get the same value from a possibly faulty input device except by having the processors communicate among themselves". And: "All that a Byzantine Generals solution can do is guarantee that all processors use the same input value."
- p.398: with median as majority/choice, "the value obtained by the nonfaulty processors lies within the range of values provided by the input unit."
- Interactive consistency conditions IC1/IC2 (§1): "IC1. All loyal lieutenants obey the same order. IC2. If the commanding general is loyal, then every loyal lieutenant obeys the order he sends."

### S3 Schneider 1984, the fail-stop processor (substitute for U2)
- Abstract: "A fail-stop processor halts instead of performing an erroneous state transformation that might be visible to other processors, can detect whether another fail-stop processor has halted (due to a failure), and has a predefined portion of its storage that will remain unaffected by failures and accessible to any other fail-stop processor."
- §1, p.146: "Stable Storage Property. The storage of a processor is partitioned into stable storage and volatile storage. The contents of stable storage are unaffected by any failure and can always be read by any processor. The contents of volatile storage are not accessible to other processors and are lost as a result of a failure."
- §1, p.146: "In some models, instead of the Failure Status Property, 'timeouts' are used to detect failures. However, use of timeouts requires the further assumption that processor clocks are synchronized."
- p.147, the property is an ideal: "Real processors do not satisfy the Halt on Failure, Failure Status, or Stable Storage properties. In fact, most real processors are not even good approximations of fail-stop processors." "it is impossible to implement a completely fault-tolerant computing system using a finite amount of hardware." The construct actually built is "a k-fail-stop processor--a collection of processors and memories that behaves like a fail-stop processor unless k + 1 or more failures occur within its components."

### S4 Lampson & Sturgis 1979, a read-outcome model for disk pages (the most read-specific source in this track)
- §4 (p.4): events are classified as "desired and undesired ... Undesired events are subdivided into expected ones, called errors, and unexpected ones, called disasters. Our algorithms are designed to work in the presence of any number of errors, and no disasters: we make no claims about their behavior if a disaster occurs." Also: "The validity of the model itself, however, cannot be established by proof ... The correctness of this claim can only be established by experience."
- §4.1 Disk storage (p.5): "Our model for disk storage is a set of addressable pages, where each page contains a status (good, bad) and a block of data." `procedure Get(at: Address) returns (status: (good, looksBad), data: Dblock)`. The results of a Get:
  - "(desired) Page a is (good, d), and Get returns (good, d)."
  - "(desired) Page a is bad, and Get returns looksBad."
  - "(error) Soft read error: Page a is good, and Get returns looksBad, provided this has not happened too often in the recent past"
  - "(disaster) Persistent read error: Page a is good, and Get returns looksBad, and nR successive Gets within a time TR have all returned looksBad."
  - "(disaster) Undetected error: Page a is bad, and Get returns good, or if page a is (good, d), then returns (good, d') with d' ≠ d."
  - The text explains: "the system must treat the page as bad if it cannot be read after repeated attempts, even though its actual status is not observable."
- Spontaneous events (§4.1, p.6): "A decay is a spontaneous event in which some set of pages, all within some one characteristic decay set, changes from good to bad." Infrequent decay is an error. "(error) Revival: a page goes from (bad, d) to (good, d)." "(disaster) Frequent decay". "(disaster) Undetected error: some page changes from (s, d) to (s, d') with d' ≠ d."
- §5.1 (p.9): "CarefulGet repeatedly does Get until it gets a good status, or until it has tried n times, where n is the bound on the number of soft read errors. This eliminates soft read errors." Stable storage has "no failure modes which must be dealt with by its clients". "A StableGet does a CarefulGet from one of the representing pages, and if the result is bad does a CarefulGet from the other one." The guarantee holds only under the no-disaster assumption: "only the desired events and the unexpected disasters for disk pages are possible for stable pages."

### S5 Gray 1985, Heisenbugs, Bohrbugs, fail-fast
- "The assertion that most production software bugs are soft Heisenbugs that go away when you look at them is well known to systems programmers. Bohrbugs, like the Bohr atom, are solid, easily detected by standard techniques". The single experiment: "In the measured period, one out of 132 software faults was a Bohrbug, the remainder were Heisenbugs." (section "Software modularity through processes and messages", spooler error-log experiment)
- Fail-fast: "the process software module be fail-fast, it should either function correctly or it should detect the fault, signal failure and stop operating." "In the terminology of [Cristian], fail-fast software has small fault detection latency."
- Evidence status: one spooler log study across "several dozen systems". This is engineering evidence, not a controlled study.

### S6 Gärtner 1999, fault classes and the four forms of fault tolerance
- §2.2: "Traditionally, faults were handled by describing the resulting behavior of the system and grouped into a hierarchic structure of fault classes or fault models [Cristian 1991; Schneider 1993a]. Wellknown examples are the crash failure model (in which processors simply stop executing at a specific point in time), fail-stop (in which a processor crashes, but this may be easily detected by its neighbors), or Byzantine (in which processors may behave in arbitrary, even malevolent, ways)." Also: "slight ambiguities or differences in definition have worked against any common understanding of even simple fault models". It quotes Cristian: "what one person calls a failure, a second person calls a fault, and a third person might call an error." (This is a secondary quotation of U1.)
- §4, Table 1: the four forms are masking (safe and live), fail-safe (safe, not live), nonmasking (live, not safe) and none. "If a program A still satisfies both its safety and its liveness properties in the presence of faults from a specified fault class F, then we say that A is masking fault tolerance for fault class F." For nonmasking, "the user may experience a certain amount of incorrect system behavior (i.e., failures). For example, a calculation result will be wrong or a replication variable may not be up to date".
- Point: fault tolerance is always defined **relative to a fault class F**.

### S7 Prabhakaran et al. 2005 (IRON), the fail-partial model and detection/recovery taxonomies
- §1: "For many years, file system and storage system designers have assumed that disks operate in a 'fail stop' manner [56]; within this classic model, the disks either are working perfectly, or fail absolutely and in an easily detectable manner."
- §2.3, the fail-partial model: "Entire disk failure: The entire disk is no longer accessible. If permanent, this is the classic 'fail-stop' failure." "Block failure: One or more blocks are not accessible; often referred to as 'latent sector errors'." "Block corruption: The data within individual blocks is altered. Corruption is particularly insidious because it is silent – the storage subsystem simply returns 'bad' data upon a read."
- §2.3.1 Transience: "failures can be 'sticky' (permanent) or 'transient' (temporary) ... a low-level media problem portends the failure of subsequent requests. In contrast, a transport or higher-level software issue might at first cause block failure or corruption; however, the operation could succeed if retried." §2.3.2 Locality: errors may show spatial locality ("a scratched surface"), or may not ("a corruption due to a misdirected write may impact only a single block").
- §2.3.3: "Reads are more problematic: if the media is unreadable, the drive has no choice but to return an error."
- §3 IRON taxonomy. Detection levels are DZero ("assumes the disk works and does not check return codes"), DErrorCode, DSanity and DRedundancy. Recovery levels are RZero, RPropagate, RStop, RGuess, RRetry, RRepair, RRemap and RRedundancy. On RGuess: "another possible reaction to a failed block read would be to manufacture a response". On RRetry: "Retry can appropriately handle transient errors, but wastes time retrying if the failure is indeed permanent." On RStop: "this recovery mechanism turns all detected disk failures into fail-stop failures".
- §5.6 empirical findings (ext3, ReiserFS, JFS, NTFS): "We found a high degree of illogical inconsistency in failure policy across all file systems". Example: "JFS will attempt to read the alternate superblock if a read failure occurs when reading the primary superblock, but it does not attempt to read the alternate if it deems the primary corrupted." §5.1: "To detect read failures, ext3 primarily uses error codes".

### S8 Huang et al. 2017, gray failure as differential observability
- §1: "gray failure: component failures whose manifestations are fairly subtle and thus defy quick and definitive detection. Examples of gray failure are severe performance degradation, random packet loss, flaky I/O, memory thrashing, capacity pressure, and non-fatal exceptions."
- Critique of the binary model (abstract/intro): fault-tolerance mechanisms "often go wrong by assuming an overly simple failure model in which a component is either correct or stopped (i.e., fail-stop)".
- §3.2 definition: "a system is defined to experience gray failure when at least one app makes the observation that system is unhealthy, but observer observes that system is healthy." Table 1 has four quadrants. "Crash and fail-stop failures fall under this case" (case 4: both observe failure).
- §3.1: "One system may be an app for another system." The model is recursive, like S1's component recursion.
- §3.3: there is a temporal evolution from "latent failure" to "gray failure" to "complete failure".
- §2.4: "a storage or network issue makes a VM unable to access its virtual disk ... the compute-cluster failure detector may incorrectly attribute the failure to the compute stack".
- Evidence status: the incidents are Azure experience. The claim that "gray failure is behind most cloud incidents" is asserted from first-hand experience and is not quantified in the paper.

### S9 Gunawi et al. 2018, fail-slow hardware
- Abstract/§1: "Fail-slow hardware is an under-studied failure mode. We present a study of 101 reports of fail-slow hardware incidents, collected from large-scale cluster deployments in 12 institutions." Definition: "hardware that is still running and functional but in a degraded mode, slower than its expected performance." Example: "disk throughput can drop by three orders of magnitude to 100 KB/s due to vibration, SSD operations can stall for seconds due to firmware bugs".
- §1 lists existing models: "fail-stop ..., fail-partial ..., fail-transient ..., faults as well as corruption ... and byzantine failures".
- §3.2 conversions: "Fail-stop, -partial, and -transient faults can convert to fail-slow faults". Fail-transient example: "disks occasionally return IO errors ... firmware/software typically masks these errors from users. A simple mechanism is to retry the operation or repair the error (e.g., with ECC or parity). However, when the transient failures are recurring much more frequently, error masking can be a 'double-edged sword'". Fail-partial: "only some part of the device is unusable (i.e., a partial fail-stop). This kind of failure is typically masked by the firmware/software layer (e.g., with remapping)."
- §3.3 symptoms: "permanent slowdown, transient slowdown (up-and-down performance), partial slowdown (degradation of sub-components), and transient stop (e.g., occasional reboots)."
- §3.5: time to detect: "1% of the cases are detected in minutes, 13% in hours, 13% in days, 11% in weeks, and 17% in months (and unknown time in 45%)."
- §4.1 SSD read path: "Read retries with different voltages: ... As flash chips wear out ... making the read operation with the default voltage threshold fail, forcing the controller to keep retrying the read with different voltage thresholds ... We observed as high as 4 retries in the field." "if the data cannot be read (i.e., is completely corrupted and fails the ECC checks), the SSD must reconstruct the page with RAIN (NAND-level RAID)". Also: "the number of bit flips is a complex function of the time since the last write, the number of reads since the last write, the temperature of the flash, and the amount of wear on the flash."
- §4.2 Disk: "a disk controller delayed I/O requests for tens of seconds". §4.3: "SRAM errors on data paths are typically transparently masked; they ultimately lead to a CRC validation error, and the network packet or disk I/O is simply retried."

### S10 Ganesan et al. 2017, single file-system faults in replicated storage
- Table 1 / §3.1 fault model for **read** operations: corruption ("zeros, junk"; causes include "misdirected and lost writes in ext and XFS") and I/O error (EIO; causes include "latent sector errors in all file systems, disk corruptions in ZFS, btrfs").
- §2.1: "ext4 returns corrupted data as-is to applications if the underlying device block is corrupted" and "btrfs and ZFS transform an underlying corruption into an error – when an underlying corrupted disk block is accessed, the application will receive an error instead of corrupted data". The same physical fault can therefore surface as content failure or as signaled failure, depending on the layer.
- §3.1: "It is possible for applications to read a block that is corrupted (with zeros or junk) if a previous write to that block was lost or some unrelated write was misdirected to that block."
- §3.2 classification: local behavior (crash, partial crash, retry, use internal redundancy, detect-and-ignore, log error, no detection) versus global effect ("global corruption, data loss, read-unavailability, write-unavailability, unavailability, or query failure").
- Expected behaviors tested include "Queries should not silently return corrupted data" and "Queries should not fail after retries".
- Abstract: "We find that modern distributed systems do not consistently use redundancy to recover from file-system faults: a single file-system fault can cause catastrophic outcomes such as data loss, corruption, and unavailability."

## 3. Existing models and classifications found (not merged; kept in source terms)
1. **Fault-error-failure chain plus 4-viewpoint service failure modes** (S1): domain {content, early timing, late timing (performance), halt (silent), erratic}; detectability {signaled, unsignaled; detector false alarm}; consistency {consistent, inconsistent = Byzantine}; consequences {minor ... catastrophic}. Fault persistence {permanent, transient}. Reproducibility {solid/hard, elusive/soft}. Intermittent faults lead to soft errors. Fail-controlled variants {fail-halt/fail-stop, fail-passive, fail-silent, fail-safe}.
2. **Byzantine / arbitrary failure plus interactive consistency** (S2), with the 3m+1 bound under oral messages.
3. **Fail-stop processor** (S3, from U2): Halt-on-Failure, Failure Status and Stable Storage properties; approximated as k-fail-stop.
4. **Desired/error/disaster event model for disk Get/Put** (S4): soft read error, persistent read error, undetected error, decay, revival; CarefulGet and StableGet abstractions.
5. **Bohrbug/Heisenbug and fail-fast** (S5).
6. **Hierarchic fault classes (crash < fail-stop ... Byzantine) and four forms of fault tolerance** (masking, fail-safe, nonmasking, none) relative to a fault class F (S6, citing U1).
7. **Fail-partial disk model** (S7): entire-disk failure, block failure (latent sector error), block corruption; sticky versus transient; locality; frequency. **IRON detection/recovery taxonomy** D{Zero, ErrorCode, Sanity, Redundancy}, R{Zero, Propagate, Stop, Guess, Retry, Repair, Remap, Redundancy}.
8. **Gray failure as differential observability** (S8): a 2x2 of system-observer versus app observation; latent, then gray, then complete failure.
9. **Fail-slow hardware** (S9): symptoms {permanent slowdown, transient slowdown, partial slowdown, transient stop}; conversions from fail-stop, fail-partial and fail-transient.
10. **File-system fault model for distributed stores** (S10): read faults {corruption (zeros/junk), EIO}; local behavior versus global effect.

## 4. Convergences
- Binary working-or-stopped is insufficient. S7 says this of disks, S8 of cloud components, S9 of hardware, S10 of replicated stores, and S3 says real processors are not fail-stop. S1 already includes partial failure and degraded modes.
- Signaled versus silent is central everywhere: S1 (signaled/unsignaled), S4 (looksBad versus undetected error), S7 ("Corruption is ... silent"), S10 (ext4 returns corrupt data; btrfs/ZFS convert it to EIO).
- Transient versus permanent and retry: S1 (transient/permanent, intermittent, soft errors), S4 (soft versus persistent read error; CarefulGet bounds retries by n), S5 (Heisenbugs go away on retry), S7 (sticky/transient, RRetry), S9 (in-device read retries with voltage shifts).
- Recursion across layers: S1 ("service failure of A appears as an external fault to B"), S8 ("One system may be an app for another system"), S10 (file system converts a device fault into a different file-system fault).
- Classification is relative to the observer or user: S1 (consistency is defined over users; failures "may require judgment"), S8 (differential observability), S2 (different readers see different values).

## 5. Contradictions and tensions
- **"Fail-stop" means different things.** In S3 (from U2), fail-stop requires halting plus detectability by others plus stable storage that "can always be read". In S1, fail-stop is a synonym for fail-halt "to an acceptable extent", which is a probabilistic design target with no stable-storage clause. S6 describes it as crash plus easy detection by neighbors. S7 calls it disks that "fail absolutely and in an easily detectable manner". So a "fail-stop disk" in S7 and a "fail-stop processor" in S3 are not the same contract.
- **Where slowness sits.** In S1, a late response is a timing (performance) failure, a failure-mode class. In S9, fail-slow is a fault type ("hardware performance faults") whose symptoms are slowdowns. In S8, slowness counts as gray failure only if the observer does not notice it. So the same slow read could be a late timing failure (S1), a fail-slow fault (S9), or a gray failure (S8), depending on the framework and the observer.
- **Masking as remedy versus hazard.** S4 builds CarefulGet/StableGet to mask soft read errors and decay, and S1 treats masking as fault tolerance. S9 reports that masking by retry or remapping becomes a "double-edged sword" that converts errors into slowness. S8 argues "A more fundamental problem with masking gray failure instead of detecting it is that failed components may not get replaced".
- **Byzantine as the safe default?** S1 (citing U3) notes that conservative Byzantine assumptions raise failure-mode coverage but need more redundancy and can lower overall dependability. S8 notes BFT "has yet to be used in a production system" and that "gray failure in most cases is not arbitrary".
- **Where error detection belongs for reads.** S7 argues that file systems must detect, with DSanity/DRedundancy. S10 finds that even with file-system checksums, distributed stores react inconsistently, often by crashing. Both are empirical, so this is less a contradiction than evidence that no layer reliably owns it.
- **Error versus disaster boundary.** S4 puts "undetected error" (wrong data with good status) in the disaster class, outside the guarantee. S7 and S10 treat silent corruption on read as a realistic, expected fault to be handled. The two use different expectation baselines, 1979 hardware assumptions versus 2005/2017 field evidence.

## 6. Gaps
- COVERAGE-GAP: Cristian 1991 (U1) was not read in full. Its omission/timing/response(value, state-transition)/crash(amnesia, partial-amnesia, pause, halting) hierarchy, and its storage-service example, are the most-cited distributed-systems failure-semantics scheme. Here they are known only secondhand (S6 and search extracts).
- COVERAGE-GAP: Powell 1992 (U3), the assumption-coverage formalism, was not read.
- COVERAGE-GAP: The normative vocabularies IEC 60050-192 and ISO/IEC/IEEE 24765 (U5, U6) were not reachable, so it is unverified whether their definitions of failure, fault and failure mode match S1.
- COVERAGE-GAP: Fail-stutter (U4) not read.
- COVERAGE-GAP: Hadzilacos & Toueg's 1994 failure hierarchy (crash, send/receive/general omission, arbitrary) was not attempted.
- GENUINE-GAP [inference]: none of the general taxonomies (S1, S2, S6) defines failure modes specific to a *stream* read, such as a short read, a partial result followed by an error mid-stream, or end-of-stream mis-signaled. S4 and S10 are per-page or per-block. The mapping from these taxonomies onto byte-stream read semantics is not given in this literature.
- GENUINE-GAP [inference]: no source here gives a standard boundary for when "late" becomes "halt" or "omission" for a read. S1 leaves the timing tolerance to the function or specification, and S3 notes that timeouts need synchronized clocks.
- GENUINE-GAP: S8 gives no quantitative prevalence for gray failure. S9's 101 reports are a convenience sample with no denominator, so they show existence and kinds, not rates.
