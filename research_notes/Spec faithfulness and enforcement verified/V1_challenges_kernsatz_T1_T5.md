Scope: verification brief §2 items 1, 2 and 5, plus an audit of the factual claims in the earlier report's §1 Kernsatz, §2.1, §2.5, §2.6 and §2.7. ## Q1 (brief §2.1): Is the Cedar "§3 challenge" spurious?

### Takeaway
Yes. The earlier brief's §3 names Cedar only as an example of "executable reference models". It never says Cedar's models are Dafny. Dafny appears in §3 only in the DbC method family and in the agent-evidence benchmarks, and in §2 as the project's own per-call contract layer. The challenge "Cedar's models are Lean, not Dafny" therefore contradicts nothing. It is a misreading of the brief by the earlier run, and V1-01 records it as **refuted**. Separately, Cedar *did* have an earlier Dafny formalization. The FSE 2024 and OOPSLA 2024 full texts never mention Dafny (0 occurrences each), but Cedar's official `cedar-spec` repository READMEs on the release/2.0.x, 2.4.x and 3.0.x branches describe "the Dafny formalization of Cedar".

### Cited Findings
- The earlier brief §3, verbatim, under "Method families surveyed": "DbC/JML/ACSL/Dafny/Larch/Spec#;" … "executable reference models (ShardStore, Cedar)." Under "Agent evidence": "agents conform well to fixed, machine-checked specs (Vericoding, DafnyBench);". The earlier brief §2, verbatim: "Dafny for per-call contracts;". No sentence links Cedar to Dafny. — `briefs/Spec faithfulness and enforcement.md` §2, §3 (repo file).
- The FSE Companion 2024 paper says "We wrote models of the Cedar evaluator, authorizer, and validator in Lean" (§3). `grep -ci dafny` on the full text returns 0. — [Disselkoen et al., FSE Companion '24, arXiv 2407.01688](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01688)
- The OOPSLA 2024 (PACMPL, DOI 10.1145/3649835) extended preprint also contains 0 Dafny mentions. — [arXiv 2403.04651](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.04651)
- The cedar-spec README on release/2.0.x says: "This repository contains the Dafny formalization of Cedar and infrastructure for performing differential randomized testing (DRT) between the formalization and Rust production implementation". Mentions on release/2.0.x are 6 Dafny and 0 Lean; on 2.4.x, 11 Dafny and 0 Lean; on 3.0.x, 11 Dafny and 1 Lean. The current main README lists only `cedar-lean`. — [cedar-spec release/2.0.x README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cedar-policy/cedar-spec/release/2.0.x/README.md); [release/2.4.x](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cedar-policy/cedar-spec/release/2.4.x/README.md); [main](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cedar-policy/cedar-spec/main/README.md)

- FSE 2024 bug counts: proofs "found and fixed 4 bugs in Cedar's policy validator, and DRT and PBT helped us find and fix 21 additional bugs in various parts of" the production code (parser, formatter, namespace handling among the examples). — [arXiv 2407.01688](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01688) §1, §4

### Inferences
- *Inference*: the earlier run seems to have conflated the project's own layer list (§2: Dafny for contracts) with the §3 example list. The correct status for the §3 point "executable reference models (ShardStore, Cedar)" is **confirmed**, with an added detail about the language history.
- *Inference*: the earlier report's §2.6(c) line "Cedar about two dozen" spec bugs is wrong in kind. The 25 bugs (4 found by proofs, 21 by DRT/PBT) are mostly bugs in the production code or the validator. None is a bug found by disagreement with prose.

### Gaps
- The date of the Dafny → Lean switch is not given in any full text read. The branch READMEs show only that 2.x and early 3.x releases shipped a Dafny formalization. The reason for the switch is not documented in the sources read.

## Q2 (brief §2.2): Does the Porcupine "§3 challenge" survive?

### Takeaway
No. Downgrade it to a **qualification**. OmniLink is a tool paper. Its authors built TLA+ trace-validation tools (TraceLink, PGo/MPCal) and here compare their own new checker against Porcupine on one system (BAT), with their own Go port of their TLA+ model. No neutral benchmark comparing Porcupine with Knossos or with OmniLink was found. Porcupine's "1,000x–10,000x" figure remains self-reported on its own `test_data/jepsen/`.

### Cited Findings
- The OmniLink authors are Hackett, Wrench, Beschastnikh and Wei (UBC) and Macko and Davis (MongoDB). Hackett & Beschastnikh authored TraceLink (cited as [22], "Finn Hackett and Ivan Beschastnikh. 2025. TraceLinking Implementations with Their Verified Designs"), and the paper links its tooling with the PGo compiler. — [OmniLink, arXiv 2601.11836](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11836) refs [22], [23]; §6
- §7.4 setup: "we translated our TLA+ model of BAT into Go, and wrote a converter … both tool outputs always agreed". The traces had 5 threads and 500 to 450k operations, plus 1–50 threads at 100 operations per thread, with 10 traces per length, each validated once per tool. — ibid. §7.4
- §7.4 results: "for small operation counts, Porcupine runs significantly faster than OmniLink, especially when the trace has many threads … Past 200k operations with few threads, however, Porcupine's performance is less consistent … takes a long time – often multiple minutes – to reject traces". On memory: "Porcupine uses significantly less memory than OmniLink for short traces … Starting at 250k action traces, however, Porcupine starts consuming significantly more memory". — ibid. §7.4, Fig. 4
- The OmniLink abstract says it found "two previously unknown bugs (1 in BAT, 1 in ConcurrentQueue)". This is *not* "in WiredTiger/BAT/ConcurrentQueue" as the earlier report's §2.3.1 table says. — ibid. abstract
- The Porcupine README says, verbatim: "Porcupine is faster and can handle more histories than Knossos's linearizability checker. Testing on the data in `test_data/jepsen/`, Porcupine is generally **1,000x**-**10,000x** faster and has a much smaller memory footprint. On histories where it can take advantage of P-compositionality, Porcupine can be millions of times faster." — [Porcupine README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/anishathalye/porcupine/master/README.md)
- Pine (IIT Delhi, A-pre) "added ∼900 lines of Go code to Porcupine" and reports that "Pine matches the execution speed of Porcupine … Pine's memory consumption was always lower". It is not neutral, since it builds on Porcupine, and it does not compare against Knossos. It cites Porcupine issue #6, which reports that Porcupine hangs on some histories. — [Pine, arXiv 2608.17388](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.17388) §1, §5.4, Fig. 9
- Horn & Kroening's P-compositionality paper (the algorithm Porcupine implements) does not benchmark Knossos in its arXiv full text. A search snippet attributes a Knossos comparison on 100 etcd histories to their 2015 group-talk slides, but that comparison pits Knossos against the slide authors' own checker. — [arXiv 1504.00204](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.00204); slides [lead only, not read](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/people/alex.horn/linearizability-tester-group-talk-2015-02-05.pdf)
- Lee & Mathur (decrease-and-conquer linearizability monitoring) has no Porcupine or Knossos mentions in its full text. — [arXiv 2410.04581](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.04581)

### Inferences
- *Inference*: replacement text for the earlier report's §2.3.1 [§3 challenge] paragraph and §2.7 row: "**[§3 qualification] Porcupine cost.** Porcupine's 1,000x–10,000x speed-up over Knossos is self-reported on its own Jepsen test data (README, B). The only head-to-head found is by authors of a competing TLA+-based checker (OmniLink, A-pre, one system, 10 traces per length). There, Porcupine was much faster on short or many-thread histories but slower and less predictable when rejecting histories of more than about 200k operations with few threads. No neutral benchmark was found. Porcupine issue #6 (cited by Pine) reports hangs on some histories. This qualifies, and does not contradict, the §3 point that history checkers have a cost: the check is NP-complete, and its cost depends on history shape."
- *Inference*: for this project (a Python reference model with short histories), the qualification hardly matters. Short histories are exactly where OmniLink found Porcupine fastest.

### Gaps
- No neutral (third-party, non-competing) Porcupine vs Knossos benchmark was found in a full text. The Oxford slides were not read.

## Q3 (brief §2.5): Is each Kernsatz claim carried at the stated strength?

### Takeaway
Partly. "About 90 % with three tests" is a real figure (100 − 9.90 = 90.1 %). It is, however, a *mean per-requirement* detection rate over only the 36 of 43 requirements for which GPT-5 produced a complete valid suite. The setting is Alloy structural domain models and wrong specs written by students; pooled over all wrong specs the rate is 88.1 %. The paper is peer-reviewed at FM 2026 (grade A, not A-pre). "LLM errors are correlated across models" holds strongly, but only in an MCQ setting (plus a résumé-screening case). "Every reference-free check is necessary-only and blind to shared misreadings" overstates the case for LLM judges, which also *reject* faithful outputs. "No published pipeline" is a search result, not a fact.

### Cited Findings
- **Cunha & Macedo setting.** The paper covers "structural requirements of simple domain models formalized in the Alloy specification language", from 4 Alloy4Fun examples with 43 requirements. Wrong specs are distinct non-equivalent student submissions: per requirement min 28, max 848, mean 157 (Table 1). The model is GPT-5 (2025-08-07) with a few-shot prompt, and RQ5 uses GPT-5 only. The prompt is "Generate N positive and N negative instances for the requirement 'Ri' for the following model", where the model is the Alloy domain model without the requirement's spec. — [Cunha & Macedo, arXiv 2510.23350v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.23350) §3, Tables 1, 6
- **What "caught" means.** "we run all 2 × N test cases of each requirement against each wrong specification and checked if the result was the expected". Only "requirements for which the LLM generates a full test suite, with 2 × N valid test cases" count, and the metric is "the average of missed wrong specifications per requirement … not the same as the ratio between Missed and Wrong". — ibid. §3.3
- **Table 6** (N, complete suites, wrong specs, missed, mean %): N=1 41/5587/2204/38.10 %; N=2 34/4623/814/17.02 %; N=3 36/4395/525/9.90 %; N=4 35/5129/446/7.45 %; N=5 35/4816/348/6.43 %. Pooled N=3 detection is 1 − 525/4395 = 88.1 %. "only in 5 requirements were all of them detected" at N=5. — ibid. Table 6, §4.5
- **Shared misreadings.** Of the 14 oracle-disagreeing tests, 13 were negatives. 9 came from "A professor cannot teach colleagues" because, "likewise many of our students, it was incorrectly assumed that 'colleagues' meant any other professor". "all 3 runs of GPT-5 made the last two mistakes". — ibid. §4.4
- **Venue.** FM 2026 Research Track, Tokyo, 18–22 May 2026. — [researchr FM 2026 page](https://conf.researchr.org/details/fm-2026/fm-2026-research-paper/14/Validating-Formal-Specifications-with-LLM-generated-Test-Cases); Semantic Scholar lists "World Congress on Formal Methods"
- **Kim et al.** The data are "Responses of 349 LLMs on 12,032 … 71 LLMs on 14,042 multiple choice questions on the Helm" (MMLU questions). "The mean agreement rate across pairs is 0.423 on HuggingFace and 0.6 on Helm, about double or higher than the baselines". The random baselines are 0.127 and 1/3. "more accurate models (and especially if both models are accurate) are more correlated". The LLM-as-judge case: "judges significantly inflate the accuracy of models from the same provider". The paper is ICML 2025, PMLR 267. — [Kim et al., arXiv 2506.07962](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.07962) §3.2, §4
- **"Necessary-only" sources.** Roundtrip: "we therefore treat formal consistency as necessary but not sufficient", and "if T1 silently drops a conjunct of x and T3 reproduces the same omission … the equivalence check passes" ([2604.25031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25031) §3, Limitations). NeuroSTAR: the violating-witness test "forms a necessary condition for the LTLf to be correct", and the generators "can produce the same wrong formula together" ([2609.03161](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03161) §IV-B, §VI-C). Clover: "if the docstring, annotations, and code all miss the same edge case, the error cannot be detected" ([Clover, arXiv 2310.17807](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17807) App. A.4.1).
- **LLM judges are not necessary-only.** A GPT-4 judge has 26.33–51.11 % precision at 88.69–94.65 % recall on FormalAlign's sets, so it over-accepts ([FormalAlign](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10135) Table 3). In Beyond Compilation, the GPT∩Gemini rule had negative confirmation 10/13 (76.9 %), so it rejects some faithful outputs ([2606.31002](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.31002) Table 2). Jin & Chen report "systematic overcorrection", i.e. rejecting correct code ([2603.00539](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.00539)).

### Inferences
- *Inference*: the Kernsatz claim "independence must come from the derivation path and the artefact rather than from swapping models" is reasoning, not a finding. Kim et al. show that *cross*-provider agreement is lower than same-provider agreement, so swapping providers buys some independence. The point holds as "not enough on its own", not as "useless".
- *Inference*: Cunha & Macedo is simultaneously the best support for prose-derived positive/negative checks *and* direct evidence of the shared-misreading limit. The LLM test writer repeated the students' misreading of "colleagues". It is the best support even though its target was student specs rather than LLM-derived specs.

### Gaps
- None of the sources measures prose-derived against spec-derived oracles head to head. The Kernsatz's third sentence stays a correct description of the evidence gap.

## Notes

- **Baseline quotes (brief §4 rule).** These are the three §3 passages the earlier report challenged or qualified within V1's scope:
  1. "executable reference models (ShardStore, Cedar)". The Cedar "challenge" contradicts nothing: §3 states no modelling language. **Refuted.**
  2. "history checkers (Elle, Porcupine, Knossos) and their cost". OmniLink does not contradict "their cost". It shows that cost depends on history shape, and it comes from competing-tool authors. **Downgraded to qualification.**
  3. "triangular cross-checks help (Clover)" and "agents conform well to fixed, machine-checked specs (Vericoding, DafnyBench)". Neither qualification contradicts the baseline. Both bound it (shared misses; weak fixed specs). **Valid qualifications**, with corrected sourcing (V1-45, V1-46).
- **Kernsatz replacement text.** This is a proposal, kept at three sentences: "The earlier run's search found no published pipeline that runs prose records → spec → formal models → executable reference model with gates that check faithfulness to the prose. The best-supported design turns each record's own scenarios and rejected options into spec-blind positive and negative checks before the spec exists. In the one measured analogue (Cunha & Macedo, FM 2026: Alloy structural models, student-written wrong specs, GPT-5), three tests of each kind detected on average about 90 % of wrong specs per requirement, for the 36 of 43 requirements with a complete valid suite. The design then conjoins all normative forms over one vocabulary to check conflicts, vacuity and satisfiability, and binds the reference model with two test families whose independence is enforced by information flow and verified by per-family mutant kills. Round-trip, dual-formalization, compile/verify and witness checks are necessary-only and blind to misreadings both legs share. LLM judges both over-accept and over-reject. In multiple-choice benchmarks, LLM errors are correlated, more so within a provider (Kim et al., ICML 2025); so, by *inference*, swapping models alone does not supply independence, and the derivation path and artefact must. The central claim, that prose-derived oracles catch spec misreadings that spec-derived ones miss, has no head-to-head measurement yet; a small trial on one operation can measure it."
- **Grade changes found by V1.** Cunha & Macedo A-pre → A (FM 2026). Jin & Chen A-pre → A (ASE journal 2026). Choi et al. A-pre → A (NeurIPS 2025). Cedar FSE in §2.6(c) A-pre → A. CodeJudgeBench may be A (ACL 2026, unconfirmed).
