## Q1. Papadakis, Shin, Yoo, Bae, ICSE 2018: mutation score vs real faults with suite size controlled

### Takeaway
Confirmed and now quantified from the full text. Mutation score correlates moderately to strongly with real-fault detection when suite size varies (mostly 0.35–0.75). The correlation falls to about 0.05–0.20 once size is held constant. Suites ranked in the top 10% or 25% by mutation score, compared with random suites of the same size, still detect significantly more faults, but only for 138 of 231 Defects4J faults and 59 of 68 CoREBench faults. Nothing in the earlier report's conclusions is reversed. The paper supports "mutation as a hole finder, not a numeric target" and qualifies the Kernsatz phrase "verified by per-family mutant kills".

### Cited Findings
- Full text read: a 12-page author/camera-ready copy carrying the ICSE '18 running header ("ICSE '18, May 27-June 3, 2018, Gothenburg, Sweden") — [typeset.io PDF](https://typeset.io/pdf/are-mutation-scores-correlated-with-real-fault-detection-a-t9e7f0t5d6.pdf). Grade A.
- Setting: CoREBench (4 C programs: Coreutils, Findutils, Grep, Make) and Defects4J (5 Java programs: JFreeChart, Closure, Commons-Lang, Commons-Math, Joda-Time). Two CoREBench faults (IDs 57, 58) were excluded. Only 231 of 357 Defects4J faults were used, "as the remaining 126 required infeasible amount of execution time". Test pools were developer tests plus generated tests (KLEE for C; Randoop and EvoSuite for Java: "20 test suites, composed of 1,375,341 test cases"). Mutation tools were the same as Just et al. and Chekam et al., with operators AOR, LOR, COR, ROR, ORU, STD, LVR (§3.1–3.4) — [same PDF](https://typeset.io/pdf/are-mutation-scores-correlated-with-real-fault-detection-a-t9e7f0t5d6.pdf).
- Method (§3.6): Kendall and Pearson correlation between MS and fault detection on 10,000 suites of random size. For the size-controlled case: "we sample 10,000 test suites per size group" at size increments of 2.5% across 0–50% of the pool.
- Coefficients (§4.3): "The results of the uncontrolled size case show moderate to strong correlations (the majority of the values is within the range 0.35 to 0.75). These correlations become relatively weak (approximately within the range 0.05 to 0.20) when the suite size is controlled … a major part of the association between mutation score and fault detection is simply an effect of size. Still a positive relation between them exists, but it is relatively weak."
- Regression (§4.2): "the fault detection cannot be predicted well by mutation scores, size or their combination". Both Size and MS are significant (p < 0.05), and Size*MS fits best.
- Mutation-ranked vs random suites of equal size (§4.4): "The Defects4J faults that have statistically significant improvements are 138, while the CoreBench are 59. This suggests that we have no evidence to support the claim that there is a significant improvement for the 40% (93 out of 231) and 13% (9 out of 68) of the Defects4J and CoreBench faults." Also: "the average improvements on fault detection for the top ranked 25% and 10% of the test suites are 8% and 11% for Defects4J and 18% and 46% for CoreBench … testers have to reach a relatively high score level before they can be confident."
- Mutant relevance (§5.1): "approximately 1% of all mutants have behaviour similarities above 0.5" to the real faults. "Overall, our results reveal that irrelevant mutants cause the weak correlations."
- Conclusion (§7): "when controlling the test suite size … all correlations become weak or moderate in the best case … mutation score is actually helpful, to testers for improving test suites (by reaching relative high levels of mutation scores), but it is not that good at representing the actual test effectiveness (real fault detection)."

### Inferences
- *Inference*: "Mutation-guided suites beat random suites" (the T4 notes' search-extract paraphrase) slightly overstates the design. The paper does not *generate* suites guided by mutants. It *ranks* randomly sampled suites by mutation score and compares the top 10%/25% with all suites of the same size. Read correctly, the finding is "at fixed size, higher mutation score → more real faults found, for most but not all faults".
- *Inference*, applied to G8 (per-family mutant kill): a kill count is evidence of *guidance*, not a measure of the family's real-fault-detection power. Compare families at equal test counts (or report kills per test), because size alone drives a large part of the apparent association. Using kills to find surviving mutants to triage (the report's "hole finder" reading) is supported. Using a kill-rate threshold as proof that a family is effective is not.
- *Inference*: the finding that about 1% of mutants resemble real faults supports the report's preference for *clause-linked* or record-linked mutants over generic operator mutants. The evidence is for code, so transfer to specs or reference models is *analogous*.

### Gaps
- The figures come from C and Java code, not from specs or executable reference models. No equivalent size-controlled study for spec or model mutants was found in this run (out of scope; Alloy4Fun remains the only model-level real-fault result cited by the earlier report).
- Per-program coefficients appear only in Figure 3 (box plots). The text extraction gives the ranges quoted above, not individual values.

## Q2. Knight & Leveson 1986; Eckhardt & Lee 1985; Littlewood & Miller 1989 (coincident failures, forced diversity)

### Takeaway
The three journal full texts remain **[unreachable]**. sunnyday.mit.edu returned 503 or reset the connection, the UVA copy returned 403, IEEE has no OA copy (Unpaywall: is_oa false for all three DOIs), and the Wayback Machine reset the connection. Two author-written NASA documents were read in full instead:
- Knight's 1986 NASA grant progress report, which restates the Knight–Leveson setup and reports correlated-fault and N-version-benefit results;
- Eckhardt & Lee's NASA TM 86369 (Jan 1985), the precursor of their TSE paper.

Together they confirm the core claim: independently developed versions fail dependently, and independence of development does not imply independence of failure. They also add a nuance the earlier report lacks: three-version systems still cut failure probability by about one order of magnitude in Knight's data. Littlewood & Miller is available as the NTRS abstract only.

### Cited Findings
- **Knight & Leveson 1986, TSE SE-12(1):96–109: [unreachable].** Tried: sunnyday.mit.edu/papers/nver-tse.pdf (503 or connection reset; WebFetch also 503), cs.virginia.edu (403), Wayback Machine (reset), UVA Libra (empty 202). Figures in the published paper remain unverified.
- Knight, "Detection of Faults and Software Reliability Analysis", Annual Progress Report, NASA grant NAG-1-605, UVA report UVA/528243/CS87/101, Aug 1986 (NTRS 19870002808). Full text read. Grade B: grant report by the experiment's author, not peer-reviewed — [NTRS PDF](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19870002808/downloads/19870002808.pdf).
  - Setup: "students in graduate and senior level classes … at the University of Virginia (UVA) and the University of California at Irvine (UCI), were asked to write programs from a single requirements specification. The result was a total of twenty-seven programs (nine from UVA and eighteen from UCI) … Each of these programs was then subjected to one million randomly-generated test cases." (§I)
  - N-version benefit despite correlation (§II): "although the faults contained in the programs were responsible for many coincident failures, there was still a substantial benefit gained from using a multi-version structure … two version systems … were able to correctly detect errors when they occurred with a probability of approximately 0.995 … For the programs in the sample, we observed a reduction in failure probabilities of approximately one order of magnitude" (three-version systems).
  - Fault nature (§IV): "A total of forty-five faults were identified in the twenty-seven programs … there are a large number of fault pairs that exhibit statistically-correlated behavior … in many cases there was no obvious similarity between faults that exhibited correlated behavior. In practice, various different and apparently unrelated faults were triggered by the same special situation in the input. It is sensitivity to special cases in the input that causes [coincident failures]".
  - Seeded faults (§VIII): 17 of the 27 programs; 408 single-fault mutants (loop bounds, if conditions, deleted assignments); 45 passed an acceptance filter; run on 45,000 of the 1M tests; pairwise χ² test at 99.5%. "within a category of seeded error, dependence among errors is common … unbiased errors do not, in general, cause independent failures."
- A KTH course document summarising Knight & Leveson gives "z-score was 100.55", "6 reported no failure whatsoever", and "two programs failed on the same test case (551) … eight programs failed on a common test case twice". It is a student/course summary, not the paper (grade D; [KTH PDF](https://www.kth.se/social/files/564df871f2765419e306178d/KnightLeveson.pdf)). **Do not cite these figures as verified.**
- **Eckhardt & Lee 1985, TSE SE-11(12):1511–1517: [unreachable]** (no NTRS download for the journal record 19870030726; IEEE closed).
- Eckhardt & Lee, NASA TM 86369, "A Theoretical Basis for the Analysis of Redundant Software Subject to Coincident Errors", Jan 1985 (NTRS 19850015006). Full text read. Grade B (NASA TM); same authors and same model as the TSE paper, whose title replaces "redundant" with "multiversion" — [NTRS PDF](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19850015006/downloads/19850015006.pdf).
  - Model assumptions (Summary): "(i) independently designed software components are chosen in a random sample and (ii) in the user environment, the system is required to execute on a stationary input series. An intensity function, called the intensity of coincident errors … describes the propensity of a population of programmers to introduce design faults in such a way that software components fail together".
  - Theorem 2 (§3.0): "a necessary and sufficient condition for (unconditional) independent failure of the component versions is that θ(x) be constant" (except on a set of usage probability 0).
  - Conclusions (§6.0): "A low coincidence of errors does not describe independence. Rather a constant intensity characterizes the case of independence and the variance of the intensity distribution measures departure from the independence model. We believe a constant intensity is a condition unlikely to hold in most applications … the effects of a high intensity of coincident errors can be much more serious to the extent of making a fault tolerant approach, on average, worse than using a single version." The model also gives an asymmetry condition (§4) under which N-version beats a single version.
- **Littlewood & Miller 1989, TSE 15(12):1596–1614: [unreachable]** (S2 openAccessPdf CLOSED; Unpaywall not OA; City repository search rate-limited, 429; no City eprint found). Abstract only, via the NTRS record: "independently developed program versions fail dependently … there is a precise duality between input choice and program choice in this model and consider a generalization in which different versions can be developed using diverse methodologies. The use of diverse methodologies is shown to decrease the probability of the simultaneous failure of several versions." — [NTRS 19900036555](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19900036555). Abstract-level only.

### Inferences
- *Inference*: Eckhardt & Lee and Knight's fault analysis locate the cause of correlation in **input difficulty**: some inputs are hard for every developer, and unrelated faults fire on the same special cases. Shared misunderstanding is not the cause. Isolating information between writers (the earlier report's "independence enforced by information flow") therefore removes one source of common-mode error, shared artefacts, but cannot by itself make failures independent. This supports the earlier report's other line, "independence must come from the derivation path and the artefact … Reid's 'describe it differently'". It also matches Littlewood & Miller's forced-diversity result (abstract-level): *different methods*, not just separate people, lower coincident failure. Transfer: *analogous*, since the evidence is about program versions, not spec or test families.
- *Inference*, for expectation tests vs spec tests: the record scenarios that are "hard" (boundary, special-case inputs) are where both families will most likely err together. The Knight data imply that hard cases deserve deliberate, differently structured treatment (e.g., explicit enumeration of special cases from the records), not just a second writer.
- *Inference*: Knight's §II finding (about 10× failure reduction for 3-version systems despite correlated faults) cuts slightly *against* reading Knight–Leveson as "diversity is useless". The evidence says the independence assumption is false, but diversity still helps, by less than independence would predict.

### Gaps
- The published Knight–Leveson figures were not verified: the number of coincident-failure tests, the expected count under independence, the test statistic and per-version failure counts. Only the NASA progress report and an unreliable KTH summary were read.
- Littlewood & Miller's model details and the "forced diversity" conditions were not checked beyond the abstract.
- The Eckhardt & Lee TSE version may differ in detail from TM 86369. Theorem numbering and wording were checked in the TM only.

## Q3. Beer, Ben-David, Eisner, Rodeh: the "20% vacuous" figure

### Takeaway
The figure is now confirmed from an original-author full text: the FMSD 2001 journal version, read in full. It is an **experience estimate from IBM hardware verification, with no n, method or time window**. It is not a measured study. The FMSD wording differs from the CAV 1997 wording quoted second-hand: "20% of *formulas* are found to be trivially valid". The same sentence adds a second figure: about 10% of non-vacuously valid formulas reveal a problem when their interesting witness is examined. The CAV 1997 original itself remains [unreachable]. Two independent citing papers quote its wording identically. No conclusion changes, but the earlier report should present this as practitioner experience from the tool's authors.

### Cited Findings
- Beer, Ben-David, Eisner, Rodeh, "Efficient Detection of Vacuity in Temporal Model Checking", Formal Methods in System Design 18, 141–163, 2001. Full text read (23 pp.). Grade A venue; the figure itself is an experience statement — [U Toronto course copy](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~chechik/courses05/csc2108/beer01.pdf).
  - §1, p. 141–142: "Several years of experience in practical formal verification of hardware at IBM [3] have shown us that during the first formal verification runs of a new hardware design, typically 20% of formulas are found to be trivially valid, and that trivial validity always points to a real problem in either the design or its specification or environment. Of the formulas which are found to be non-trivially valid, examination of a non-trivial example trace discovers a problem for approximately 10% of the formulas." Ref [3] is Beer, Ben-David, Eisner, Landver, "RuleBase: An industry-oriented formal verification tool", i.e. the authors' own tool.
  - Vacuity covers more than antecedent failure (abstract): "propositional logic formulas containing implications can suffer from antecedent failure … We call this vacuity, and extend the definition to cover other kinds of trivial validity." The practical method covers the w-ACTL subset; "Most of the formulas written by users are w-ACTL formulas" (§5.1).
- CAV 1997, LNCS 1254:279–290 (DOI 10.1007/3-540-63166-6_28): **[unreachable]** (S2 openAccessPdf CLOSED; Unpaywall not OA). Its wording as quoted by Post, Hoenicke, Podelski, RE 2011 (full text read): "our experience has shown that typically 20% of specifications pass vacuously during the first formal-verification runs of a new hardware design, and that vacuous passes always point to a real problem in either the design or its specification or environment", citing "[3] … CAV, 1997, pp. 279–290" — [Post et al. RE 2011 author copy](https://jochen-hoenicke.de/docs/php11-re.pdf). This matches the Kupferman–Vardi quote used by the earlier report.

### Inferences
- *Inference*: the figure is a rule of thumb from hardware model checking with IBM RuleBase. It has no sample size and was reported by the tool's builders. It motivates a vacuity gate (G5) but does not *measure* how often LLM-written or library-API specs are vacuous. For that, the earlier report already cites measured, though A-pre, evidence (TLA+-Bench: 5 of 18 testable passes survived invariant mutation). Transfer: *analogous*.
- *Inference*: the second figure ("~10% of non-trivially valid formulas" reveal a problem via an interesting witness) supports the report's "interesting witnesses" step under G5, at the same strength, i.e. experience, not measurement.

### Gaps
- The CAV 1997 text itself was not read. Its wording is confirmed only through two independent quotations, which agree verbatim.

## Q4. Heitmeyer, Jeffords, Labaw TOSEM 1996 (SCR consistency checker); Selby, Basili, Baker TSE 1987 (Cleanroom)

### Takeaway
**Heitmeyer**: full text read. On the revised A-7 OFP requirements, which two review teams had checked, the checker reported 19 Coverage findings in 36 condition tables (17 real errors) and 57 Disjointness findings in 3 mode-transition tables of 700 rows ("a few probably are not" errors). The whole analysis took 245 s. This confirms the T2 notes' snippet claim ("found errors human reviewers missed") and supports the report's case-table complete/disjoint check. The evidence comes from the tool's authors evaluating their own tool on one specification.

**Selby et al.**: full text (scanned) read on pages 1, 2, 4, 5, 9 and 10 of 11. **Both arms received the same independent statistical testing.** The study therefore isolates Cleanroom's non-execution development plus formal methods, *not* the independence of testing. The earlier T3b note listed "separate teams (Cleanroom statistical testing …)" as an independence mechanism "with any evidence". Selby et al. does not support that. The report body does not rely on Selby, so no report conclusion changes.

### Cited Findings
- Heitmeyer, Jeffords, Labaw, "Automated Consistency Checking of Requirements Specifications", ACM TOSEM 5(3):231–261, July 1996. Full text read (publisher-typeset copy on a Konkuk University course page). Grade A — [Konkuk copy](http://dslab.konkuk.ac.kr/class/2010/10RE/Automated%20Consistency%20Checking%20of%20Requirements%20Specification.pdf). (The DTIC copy ADA465574 returned 403, as did ACM DL.)
  - §5: the subject was the revised A-7 Operational Flight Program requirements (Alspaugh et al. 1992); "The OFP contains more than 16K lines of tight assembly code". Cost: "Running on a Sun SPARCstation 20 … The tool required a total computation time of 245 seconds to perform the entire analysis. This time includes a time of 45 seconds to check the syntax and type correctness of the tables and only 200 seconds for all of the Disjointness and Coverage checks, 30 seconds for the condition tables, and 170 seconds for the mode transition tables."
  - §5.1: "our tool checked 36 condition tables, a total of 98 rows, for both Coverage and Disjointness. The tool found 19 errors. Seventeen of these, distributed over 11 tables, proved to be legitimate errors … all 17 are Coverage errors … the information needed to detect Disjointness errors is in the table, whereas the information needed to detect Coverage errors is not. Finding Coverage errors requires knowledge of all values a variable can take on … in several cases, it [type information] is omitted entirely."
  - §5.2: "The three mode classes contain a total of 48 different modes … The mode transition tables together contain a total of 700 rows … our tool found 57 Disjointness errors. Although many of the 57 instances … are undoubtedly errors, a few probably are not, since some detected events may be impossible … Many of the Disjointness errors in the table for the Weapons mode class were instances of nondeterminism present in the prose requirements documents".
  - §5.3: "Prior to its publication, the revised A-7 requirements document was carefully reviewed by two teams … our tools detected many significant errors that the reviewers missed." Also: "Not only are tools more effective than people for checking these properties; in addition, they can significantly reduce a labor-intensive task".
  - Internal inconsistency on the Darlington comparator: §1 says "the high cost (several million dollars) of the Darlington certification effort", while §5.3 says "the Darlington certification effort, which cost over U.S. $40M". In either case the cost comparison is argued, not measured.
- Selby, Basili, Baker, "Cleanroom Software Development: An Empirical Evaluation", IEEE TSE SE-13(9):1027–1037, Sept 1987. Scanned author copy, read visually on pages 1, 2, 4, 5, 9, 10 of 11. Grade A — [Basili UMD J33 PDF](http://www.cs.umd.edu/~basili/publications/journals/J33.pdf).
  - Abstract: "15 three-person teams developed versions of the same software system (800–2300 source lines); ten teams applied Cleanroom, while five applied a more traditional approach."
  - §IV-D, p. 1030: "**Note that both the Cleanroom and non-Cleanroom teams had the benefit of the independent testing throughout development.** An independent party would apply statistically based testing to each of the deliveries and report to the team members both the successful and unsuccessful test cases." §IV-E: test data came from an operational profile built by polling eleven users; "The operational statistics … were calculated from 50 user-session test cases run on the final system release of each team." The tester was "an independent party (i.e., R. W. Selby)", one of the authors.
  - The manipulated variable (p. 1030): Cleanroom teams "were not able to execute their programs … relied on the techniques of code reading, structured walkthroughs, and inspections"; non-Cleanroom teams "were able to execute and debug their programs".
  - Assignment (p. 1030): not randomised. "we decided to have all the members of one course apply the same development approach … Cleanroom would be used in the earlier (1982) course"; footnote 2: "This decision also happened to result in the two groups not being as close in terms of size as they could have been."
  - Results (Fig. 4, 5, 13; one-tailed Mann–Whitney): implementation completeness 82.5 vs 60.0 (p = .088); successful operational tests without duplicate failures 92.5% vs 80.8% (p = .055); with duplicate failures 78.7% vs 59.2% (p = .134). Footnote 6: comparing the best 60% of each group gives .045 and .034. "Since both groups of teams had independent testing of all their deliveries, the early testing of deliveries must have revealed most faults overlooked by the Cleanroom developers" (p. 1031).
  - Conclusions (p. 1035): "the ideas in Cleanroom help attain the goals of producing high quality software … The complete separation of development from testing appears to cause a modification in the developers' behavior". On transfer: "a relatively small project—the direct extrapolation of the findings to other projects and development environments is not implied" (p. 1036).

### Inferences
- *Inference*, for the report's case-table row ("Per-table complete/disjoint check (ms–s, DMN)", §4 table) and the brief §2 "case table, which must be complete and disjoint": Heitmeyer adds an older, A-graded industrial-specification data point that complete/disjoint checks on tables find errors human reviewers missed, at seconds-scale cost. Transfer: *analogous* (avionics requirements tables, not library API specs). Independence flag: tool authors evaluating their own tool, n = 1 specification.
- *Inference*: Heitmeyer's observation that Coverage errors need **type information that lies outside the table** directly supports the report's "one shared vocabulary / signature" requirement. A completeness check is only as good as the declared value domains, so the domain declarations must be part of the checked artefact.
- *Inference*: Heitmeyer reports that many A-7 nondeterminism errors were "present in the prose requirements documents". This is a faithfulness-relevant point: a table check found defects inherited from the prose, not introduced by the translation. A pipeline that assumes the records are complete should still expect some record-level contradictions to surface at G3.
- *Inference*, for Selby: the study cannot speak to "independent testing as an independence mechanism", because both arms had it. The effect it shows is that developers barred from executing code wrote more complete products, at one-tailed p between .055 and .134, with n = 15 non-randomised student teams. Anyone citing Cleanroom as evidence for independent test families should drop it, or cite it only for "statistical operational testing by a separate party was feasible", which is descriptive.

### Gaps
- Selby: pages 3, 6, 7, 8 and 11 were not read (scanned PDF, visual reading only). They hold the goal framework, static code measures, process measures and the reference list. The figures and conclusions above come from pages 1, 2, 4, 5, 9 and 10.
- Heitmeyer: no false-positive rate for the 57 Disjointness findings is given ("a few probably are not").
