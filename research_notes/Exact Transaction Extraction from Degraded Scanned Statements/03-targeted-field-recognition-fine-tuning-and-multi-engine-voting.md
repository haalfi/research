Rationale: Academic and practitioner evidence for font-specific fine-tuning on synthetic renders, cropping cells for per-field re-recognition with restricted character sets, and combining multiple engines/binarizations by voting (Calamari voting, ROVER, ISRI studies) as both an accuracy gain and a disagreement-based error detector.

## S12: https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.09670

Source quality (reader's rating): primary. Date: 2017-11-27 (arXiv v1); published at DAS 2018, DOI 10.1109/DAS.2018.30.

- **S12.0** (central; voted 3-0, confirmed): Training five OCRopus models on different cross-validation folds of the same ground truth (150 lines) and combining their outputs by plain majority voting (ISRI tools) cut character errors on unseen lines by 8-35% relative to the best single model and 26-62% relative to the worst, on all seven early printed books tested; adding per-character confidences plus top-n alternatives removed roughly another 5-10% of errors (e.g. book 1476: single models 3.32-4.07% CER, ISRI voting 2.21%, confidence voting 1.82%). Evidence is for book-specific LSTM models on historical print, not for Tesseract or bank statements, so transfer to this task is a hypothesis.
  - Quote: "The amount of errors corrected by combining the outputs varies from 16% for the best individual models to 62% for the worst ones. All improvements are highly significant on a better than 0.001 level with the χ2 test. Incorporating the confidence information approximately reduces the amount of errors by another 5% to 10%."
- **S12.1** (central; voted 3-0, confirmed): Voting cannot recover characters that are missing or so degraded that every model misreads them; the gain from voting shrinks as models approach perfection because the residual errors are of this unrecoverable kind. This bounds what multi-model voting can do for 'missing information in the image' failures and implies such cases need detection/flagging rather than correction.
  - Quote: "This has to be expected for models that get closer to perfection as most of the remaining errors are unavoidable ones such as missing characters or highly degraded glyphs leading to misrecognition by any model. Both of these cases cannot be restored by voting."
- **S12.2** (supporting; extracted from the source by a reader agent; not voted in round 1): Voting remains effective at high baseline accuracy: on the three books whose models exceeded 99% character accuracy, confidence voting still removed at least one third of the remaining errors (e.g. 1488 with 1000 lines: 0.65% CER after voting, 34% improvement over the average single model).
  - Quote: "It is especially noteworthy that voting is still very effective when combining the output of very high performance models: On all three books that surpassed a character accuracy rate of 99% the average amount of remaining errors was reduced by at least one third."
- **S12.3** (supporting; extracted from the source by a reader agent; not voted in round 1): A simple majority vote can be wrong when most models share the same misreading of a degraded glyph; in the paper's worked example a degraded 'e' was read as 'c' by three of five models, and only summing confidences of the top-1 character and its alternatives (alternatives with confidence > 1%) selected the correct character. Disagreement between models is also proposed as an uncertainty signal for selecting lines needing attention.
  - Quote: "Therefore, the simple majority vote leads to a c in the final output as does the confidence voting when only considering the actually recognized character. However, when incorporating the alternatives the correct solution e is chosen."
- **S12.4** (supporting; extracted from the source by a reader agent; not voted in round 1): Individually trained models on the same ground truth vary strongly in error rate (one model producing up to 80% more errors than another), and without held-out ground truth one cannot tell whether a single training run went well; the ensemble is robust to one flawed model. Compute cost is CPU-feasible: 5 folds x 150 lines took 177 min training plus 26 min recognition of a 4651-line book on a quad-core i5-6300HQ laptop with 8 GB RAM.
  - Quote: "Furthermore, it is worth mentioning that the CERs of the individually trained models vary strongly, producing up to 80% more errors. This kind of variance represents a big problem for the standard approach. Obviously, in a real world scenario there is no way to determine if the training of a single model went well in terms of variance."

## S13: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.00052

Source quality (reader's rating): primary. Date: 2022-07-24 (arXiv v2; v1 submitted 2022-03-31).

- **S13.0** (central; voted 3-0, confirmed): Small per-character error rates compound into near-certain field-level errors in numeric tables: at 95% character accuracy, a table of ten six-digit numbers contains at least one error in 95.4% of cases (binomial model, 60 trials). This is the paper's own back-of-envelope calculation, not a measurement.
  - Quote: "applying an OCR engine with a 95 percent character accuracy rate on a table with ten six-digit numbers will result in an output with errors in 95.4 percent of cases."
- **S13.1** (central; voted 3-0, confirmed): Multi-engine ensembles voting at word level, then at character level on right-aligned numbers, can recover a correct number even when every individual engine misreads it; this only helps if engines' errors are idiosyncratic rather than shared and each engine at least locates the token. The paper supports this with a worked illustrative example and the authors' experience ('From experience, this seems to be the case'), not with a reported quantitative ablation.
  - Quote: "it could also be the case that the true value is 123 but the engines instead identify it as 23, 120, and 153. Here, no single value wins the vote, so we can right-align the values and have the engines vote at the character level [...] even though no single OCR engine correctly identified the number 123, the ensemble of the three engines still voted for the correct number."
- **S13.2** (central; extracted from the source by a reader agent; not voted in round 1): Rule-based field validation and correction (format invariants such as no leading zero or wrong digit grouping; letter-to-digit substitutions like O->0 in numeric fields) is recommended, but substitutions can introduce false positives and each rule should be benchmarked against ground truth before adoption.
  - Quote: "Note, however, that there is a cost to these replacements in that they might introduce false positives, so users should avoid being too aggressive with them and always try to benchmark how the addition of each replacement affects the quality of the final dataset."
- **S13.3** (supporting; extracted from the source by a reader agent; not voted in round 1): Binarization can destroy information that OCR engines and human reviewers could use on low-quality scans, so grayscale vs. monochrome input must be decided empirically per document; among five binarization methods tried (fixed threshold, Otsu, adaptive mean, Sauvola, Wolf), the authors found Sauvola and Wolf most robust on historical documents, and recommend CLAHE for grayscale contrast correction. The comparison is shown qualitatively in figures, without OCR accuracy numbers.
  - Quote: "binarization, the process of converting images to black and white, often produces poor results with low-quality scans. This is because forcing each pixel to take binary values eliminates information that both OCR engines and human reviewers might be able to use otherwise to better recognize the images."
- **S13.4** (supporting; extracted from the source by a reader agent; not voted in round 1): A pipeline augmented with pre-processing, validation against invariants (accounting identities, numeric format constraints) and a flag-driven side-by-side review interface cut human review time on the OCC balance-sheet tables from about 20 minutes per page (manual entry) and 8 minutes (off-the-shelf OCR plus manual correction) to about 1 minute per page; the authors also advise holding out part of the ground truth for cross-validation to avoid overfitting tuned parameters. The time figures are the authors' approximate self-reported estimates on one dataset using cloud OCR engines (Google/Amazon), not a controlled experiment, and no residual undetected-error rate is reported.
  - Quote: "this approach reduced the review time of the OCC bank balance sheet to about a minute per page—with some pages requiring extensive corrections but most requiring minimal review."

## S14: https://github.com/tesseract-ocr/tessdoc/blob/main/tess4/TrainingTesseract-4.00.md

Source quality (reader's rating): primary. Date: not stated.

- **S14.0** (central; extracted from the source by a reader agent; not voted in round 1): Tesseract's official training guide states that fine-tuning an existing LSTM model on task-specific data may work with only a small amount of training data when the problem is close to the existing training data (e.g. an unusual font), whereas training from scratch without a large representative set is likely to over-fit.
  - Quote: "Fine tune. Starting with an existing trained language, train on your specific additional data. This may work for problems that are close to the existing training data, but different in some subtle way, like a particularly unusual font. May work with even a small amount of training data."
- **S14.1** (central; extracted from the source by a reader agent; not voted in round 1): In the guide's worked example, fine-tuning the full tessdata_best English model on a single new font (Impact) for 400 iterations brought training-set error to 0.533%/1.633% char/word (0.017%/0.120% in a stand-alone test on the same training data) while base-set error was only slightly worse at 0.255% char / 0.825% word; training beyond 400 iterations raised base-set error. The result is on synthetic rendered text, not degraded scans.
  - Quote: "It is only slightly worse, despite having reached close to zero error on the training set, and achieved it in only 400 iterations. **Note that further training beyond 400 iterations makes the error on the base set higher.**"
- **S14.2** (central; extracted from the source by a reader agent; not voted in round 1): A few new characters can be added to an existing model's character set by fine-tuning with very few samples: 14 inserted instances of the plus-minus sign in the training text and 3600 iterations were enough for the model to recognise every plus-minus sign in a held-out font (Impact), with 2.38% char / 8.38% word error on that held-out font overall.
  - Quote: "This is excellent news! It means that one or more new characters can be added without impacting existing accuracy, **and** the ability to recognize the new character will, to some extent at least, generalize to other fonts!"
- **S14.3** (supporting; extracted from the source by a reader agent; not voted in round 1): Fine-tuning (for a font or for added characters) can only start from the float models in tessdata_best, not from the integer models in tessdata or tessdata_fast, so an offline pipeline that ships the default or fast models must additionally obtain the best model to fine-tune.
  - Quote: "Please note that Fine Tuning for Impact can be done ONLY by using `float` models as the base to continue from i.e. use the traineddata files from [tessdata_best](https://github.com/tesseract-ocr/tessdata_best) repo not from [tessdata](https://github.com/tesseract-ocr/tessdata) or [tessdata_fast](https://github.com/tesseract-ocr/tessdata_fast)."
- **S14.4** (supporting; extracted from the source by a reader agent; not voted in round 1): The guide warns that the number of fine-tuning iterations must be tuned because excessive training on a small data set over-fits, and that LSTM training data only needs line-level boxes (transcribed text lines), which makes building training data from existing images far easier than for the legacy engine.
  - Quote: "NOTE: When fine tuning, it is important to experiment with the number of iterations, since excessive training on a small data set will cause over-fitting."

## S15: https://dblp.org/rec/conf/drr/LundKR13

Source quality (reader's rating): primary. Date: 2013-02-04.

- **S15.0** (central; voted 3-0, confirmed): ACCESS NOTE: the assigned dblp URL was unreachable (Anubis bot-protection 'Access Denied'), and the SPIE full text is closed access. All claims below rest only on the paper's abstract, retrieved verbatim from the OpenAlex record for DOI 10.1117/12.2006228 (metadata cross-checked against Crossref: Lund, Kennard, Ringger; SPIE Proc. 8658, 86580R). Treat them as abstract-level evidence, not full-text-verified. The method claim: instead of choosing one binarization, the same grayscale image is thresholded at several simple global threshold values, each version is OCRed, and the OCR outputs are combined.
  - Quote: "Instead of relying on any one imperfect binarization technique, our method incorporates information from multiple simple thresholding binarizations of the same image to improve text output."
- **S15.1** (central; voted 0-3, refuted): Combination is done at the text level by aligning the OCR outputs of the differently thresholded images into a word lattice of alternatives. The lattice (oracle) word error rate was 7.6% with two thresholds and 6.8% with five, i.e. the correct word is present among the alternatives far more often than in any single OCR output (baseline WER 13.8% or higher), and most of the oracle gain comes from the second threshold.
  - Quote: "Our results show a LWER of 7.6% when aligning two threshold images and a LWER of 6.8% when aligning five."
- **S15.2** (central; voted 1-2, refuted): After committing to a single hypothesis from the lattice (using the selection method of Lund et al. 2011), the achieved WER was 8.41%, versus 13.8% or higher for single-binarization OCR on the same corpus. The realized result (8.41%) is worse than the lattice oracle bound (6.8%), so automatic selection does not recover all of the information the lattice contains.
  - Quote: "From the word lattice we commit to one hypothesis by applying the methods of Lund et al. (2011) achieving an improvement over the original OCR output and a 8.41% WER result on this data set."
- **S15.3** (supporting; extracted from the source by a reader agent; not voted in round 1): The evidence comes from a single corpus of 19th-century newspaper grayscale images with known transcriptions, with word error rate as the metric and one unnamed (in the abstract) 'state-of-the-art' OCR engine as of 2013. It is not evidence about modern LSTM engines, tabular financial documents, numeric fields or punctuation, so transfer to exact-field accuracy on scanned card statements is a hypothesis, not a published result.
  - Quote: "Using a new corpus of 19th century newspaper grayscale images for which the text transcription is known, we observe WERs of 13.8% and higher using current binarization techniques and a state-of-the-art OCR engine."
- **S15.4** (supporting; extracted from the source by a reader agent; not voted in round 1): The authors frame binarization as the step that decides which pixels are foreground and therefore as a source of OCR error on noisy documents, motivating treating the threshold as a variable to be sampled rather than fixed. This supports a diagnostic experiment for the statement pipeline: re-OCR the same grayscale scan at several thresholds and treat disagreement between outputs as a preprocessing-sensitivity signal.
  - Quote: "Since image binarization is often the method used to identify foreground pixels, a body of research seeks to improve image-wide binarization directly."
