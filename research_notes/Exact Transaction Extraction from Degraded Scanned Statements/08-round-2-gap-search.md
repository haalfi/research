## G1-confidence

- **G1-confidence.1** (access: full_text; evidence: source_code): Tesseract's word confidence (hOCR x_wconf, TSV conf column) is not a separately trained probability: for both engines it is the internal word 'certainty' mapped linearly as clip(100 + 5*certainty, 0, 100). hOCR truncates it to an integer; TSV prints the float. The formula is identical in tags 4.0.0, 4.1.1, 5.0.0 and 5.5.0 (checked by grep on each tag).
  - Source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tesseract-ocr/tesseract/main/src/ccmain/ltrresultiterator.cpp
  - Quote: "// The number should be interpreted as a percent probability. (0.0f-100.0f) [...] mean_certainty /= certainty_count; return ClipToRange(100 + 5 * mean_certainty, 0.0f, 100.0f);"
  - Relevance: Establishes what the baseline's only built-in error signal actually is: a clipped linear rescaling of an internal score. The 'percent probability' wording is a code comment, not a calibration guarantee.
  - Caveats: Read on main (VERSION 5.5.4) plus spot-greps on release tags. hOCR cast: src/api/hocrrenderer.cpp `x_wconf " << static_cast<int>(res_it->Confidence(RIL_WORD))`; TSV: src/api/baseapi.cpp `std::to_string(res_it->Confidence(RIL_WORD))`. Official tessdoc has no LSTM-specific explanation; tess3/FAQ-Old.md 'How is confidence calculated?' describes only the legacy engine (certainty = -20 * distance; word certainty = min over characters).
- **G1-confidence.2** (access: full_text; evidence: source_code): Under the LSTM engine a word's certainty is the WORST character's certainty (minimum natural-log network probability over that character's timesteps, floor -20), with a -0.085 offset, multiplied by 2.25 on non-dictionary beam paths, then min'ed with the adjacent space certainty and multiplied by 7. INFERENCE from composing these code paths: x_wconf = 100 + 35*c, so a dictionary-path word tops out at about 97 and a non-dictionary-path word at about 93, and x_wconf reaches 0 once the weakest timestep probability falls to roughly 0.06 (dictionary path) or roughly 0.31 (non-dictionary path).
  - Source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tesseract-ocr/tesseract/main/src/lstm/recodebeam.h
  - Quote: "The conversion to Tesseract rating and certainty uses the sum of the log probs (log of the product of probabilities) for the Rating and the minimum log prob for the certainty"
  - Relevance: x_wconf is a worst-character score entangled with dictionary status, not a per-word correctness probability. Amounts, dates and merchant strings may be scored on a different scale from dictionary words, so one global threshold treats them unequally.
  - Caveats: Constants read in other files: src/ccmain/linerec.cpp `const float kCertaintyScale = 7.0f;` and `word_certainty = std::min(word->space_certainty, word->best_choice->certainty()); word_certainty *= kCertaintyScale;`; src/lstm/lstmrecognizer.cpp `kDictRatio = 2.25`, `kCertOffset = -0.085`; src/lstm/recodebeam.cpp `cert * dict_ratio` on the non-dawg heap; networkio.cpp `kMinCertainty = -20.0f`. The numeric caps (97/93) and zero points are my arithmetic, not stated in any doc; they ignore the top-2 rule that adds the null probability to a character's probability. I did not check which dawgs (word/number/punctuation) count as 'dictionary path' for digits. Independent corroboration of the cap: Gilbey & Schoenlieb (arXiv 2105.04515) observe 'Tesseract appears to never give a confidence score greater than 97'.
- **G1-confidence.3** (access: full_text; evidence: source_code): With hocr_char_boxes=1 the hOCR output adds one ocrx_cinfo span per character carrying x_bboxes and x_conf, where x_conf = clip(100 + 5*character certainty). INFERENCE from the code: the x7 word scaling is applied only to the word-level certainty, so per-character x_conf is on a 7-times flatter scale than x_wconf (a timestep probability of 0.5 on a dictionary path gives x_conf of about 96 but x_wconf of about 73), and the two must not share a threshold.
  - Source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tesseract-ocr/tesseract/main/src/api/hocrrenderer.cpp
  - Quote: "hocr_str << "\n       <span class='ocrx_cinfo' title='x_bboxes " << left << " " << top << " " << right << " " << bottom << "; x_conf " << res_it->Confidence(RIL_SYMBOL) << "'>";"
  - Relevance: Per-character confidences are available offline for digit-level checks on amounts, but they are compressed near 100 and need their own threshold; a naive 'x_conf < 90' rule would flag almost nothing.
  - Caveats: Scale difference is my inference: linerec.cpp calls `word->best_choice->set_certainty(word_certainty)` only (word level), while RIL_SYMBOL reads `best_choice->certainty(blob_index_)`; I found no other use of kCertaintyScale by grep but did not run the binary. The 96/73 example is my arithmetic. The main-branch man page (doc/tesseract.1.asc) warns: 'character-level bounding boxes may be less precise when using the LSTM engine, because LSTM operates at the line level and character positions are approximated from the line result'. Those parameter descriptions are on main only; `grep` found none in the 5.5.0 tag's man page. hocr_char_boxes exists from 4.1.0 ('Add character boxes in hOCR output' in the 4.1.0 release notes).
- **G1-confidence.4** (access: full_text; evidence: source_code): lstm_choice_mode=1 adds, per timestep, every raw network output >= 0.01 as x_confs = int(p*100); lstm_choice_mode=2 adds per-character alternatives obtained by re-running the beam search (lstm_choice_iterations, default 5) with earlier best codes excluded, scored as x_confs = clip(100 - lstm_rating_coefficient * rating), rating being the summed negative log-probability. lstm_rating_coefficient (default 5) is present in 5.0.0 and 5.5.0 but absent from 4.1.1.
  - Source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tesseract-ocr/tesseract/main/src/ccmain/tesseractclass.cpp
  - Quote: "With 1 the alternative symbol choices per timestep are included. With 2 alternative symbol choices are extracted from the CTC process instead of the lattice. The choices are mapped per character."
  - Relevance: These are the only built-in ways to get alternative readings (e.g. 8 vs 3, 1 vs 7) from Tesseract for a second-opinion check on amounts; mode 1 exposes the least-processed probabilities, mode 2 a heuristic score with a tunable zero cut-off.
  - Caveats: Quote is the parameter help string (adjacent C string literals joined). Mechanics read in src/lstm/recodebeam.cpp (SaveMostCertainChoices: `if (outputs[i] >= 0.01f)`; extractSymbolChoices), src/lstm/lstmrecognizer.cpp, ltrresultiterator.cpp (`confidence = 100 - rating_coefficient_ * choice.second;`). Help text for the coefficient: 'The smaller the coefficient, the better are the ratings for each choice and less information is lost due to the cut off at 0.' The public header comment for ChoiceIterator::Confidence says the LSTM range is '0.0f - 1.0f' and choices 'should roughly add up to 1.0f', which contradicts the code (0-100, no normalisation); the tessdoc APIExample shows mode-2 values 83/16 and 54/17/15/6/4/1/1. No accuracy or calibration evaluation of these alternatives was found. A PR #2294 comment (read only through a page summariser) says only best-path scores are reliable.
- **G1-confidence.5** (access: full_text; evidence: documentation): tessedit_char_whitelist / tessedit_char_blacklist became effective for the LSTM engine in Tesseract 4.1.0 (released 2019-07-07); in 4.0.0 they affected only the legacy engine. Source check: src/lstm/recodebeam.cpp contains no get_enabled() test at tag 4.0.0 and contains one at tags 4.1.0, 4.1.1, 5.0.0, 5.3.4 and 5.5.0.
  - Source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tesseract-ocr/tessdoc/main/ReleaseNotes.md
  - Quote: "# V4.1.0 _Jul 07 2019_ [...] * Implemented support for whitelist/blacklist in LSTM engine."
  - Relevance: Fixes the minimum Tesseract version for any whitelist-based amount/date re-read pass in the offline pipeline; distro builds of 4.0.x silently ignore the setting under --oem 1.
  - Caveats: Same line appears in the archived ChangeLog ('2019-07-07 - V4.1.0 ... Implemented support for whitelist/blacklist in LSTM engine', commit 64eab6c). The implementing change is PR #2294 (patch read: commits by Robert Schubert dated 7-14 Mar 2019). Issue #751 history (4.00alpha report, 'not supported for LSTM') was read only through a page summariser. The current main man page tags both parameters '[Both]' engines.
- **G1-confidence.6** (access: full_text; evidence: source_code): The LSTM whitelist is a decode-time filter, not a retrained or renormalised classifier: the beam search skips any continuation whose decoded character is disabled, and lower-ranked classes are tried only if the beam would otherwise be empty. INFERENCE: because the CTC null (blank) continuation is never disabled, a timestep whose top character is disallowed can be absorbed by null, so a glyph can be dropped from the output instead of being replaced by the best allowed character; confidences are still the unrenormalised network outputs.
  - Source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tesseract-ocr/tesseract/main/src/lstm/recodebeam.cpp
  - Quote: "// Work through the scores by group (top-2, top-n, the rest) while the beam is empty. [...] so we fall back to the rest if the beam is empty. [...] continue; // disabled by whitelist/blacklist"
  - Relevance: A digits-and-punctuation whitelist on amount columns can turn a substitution error (visible, checkable) into a deletion (a shorter but plausible number), which is worse for exact extraction; whitelisted re-reads need a length/geometry or checksum cross-check.
  - Caveats: The dropping behaviour is my reading of DecodeStep/ContinueContext, not a documented statement and not tested by me on images. Supporting but weaker evidence: PR #2294 commit message (patch read verbatim): 'block context continuations which emit final codes translating to disabled unichar_ids. (The normal logic for fallback from top2 > top2 > rest will apply.)'; a PR comment attributed to the author, 23 Mar 2019, obtained only via a page summariser: 'if only digits are allowed, no alternatives besides null appear in the beam, because it is too narrow'. No official doc page states any LSTM whitelist caveat; the main man page only says listed characters 'will never appear in the output'. Whitelist membership is resolved through the model's unicharset (UNICHARSET::set_black_and_whitelist), so characters absent from the model cannot be enabled.
- **G1-confidence.7** (access: full_text; evidence: measured): A measured reliability curve exists for Tesseract 4.1.1 LSTM word confidence on degraded input: on 20,000 synthetic text lines rendered at 60 dpi and upscaled, the fraction of correct words is strongly non-linear in confidence, and the authors had to remap it (c<80: 0.5c+30; c>=80: 1.7c-65), i.e. low-confidence words are right far more often than stated (about 30% correct at confidence 0, about 55% at 50). The same paper asserts, without showing data, that 300 dpi confidences are a fairly good probability estimate.
  - Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.04515
  - Quote: "while the word confidence scores for 300 dpi images turn out to be a fairly good estimate of the probability of the word being correctly identified, this is not the case for 60 dpi images."
  - Relevance: Closest measured evidence that Tesseract LSTM confidence calibration shifts with scan degradation and font, which is exactly the regime of the target statements; a threshold tuned on clean scans will not transfer.
  - Caveats: Gilbey & Schoenlieb, arXiv 10 May 2021. Modified Tesseract 4.1.1 with LSTM models retrained for low-resolution input, synthetic lines from Tesseract training text, English prose, not statements or numerals. Figure 8 is a plot only (no table, no ECE, no precision/recall); the percentages I give are computed from their piecewise formula, which they call 'a very rough approximation'. They also report 'a lot of variation in this between different fonts (not shown here)'. The 300 dpi statement has no accompanying figure or numbers.
- **G1-confidence.8** (access: full_text; evidence: measured): At aggregate level, Tesseract 4 word confidence tracks word error rate closely: across three ground-truth sets of historical prints, a linear fit of WER on word confidence gave Pearson r = -0.942 (OCR-D-GT), -0.949 (VEB, 75 newspaper pages) and -0.838 (OCR-D-GT-VD-SBB, 348 pages), with linear R2 of 0.877, 0.900 and 0.649. This supports using mean confidence to rank pages or documents by quality, not to decide whether an individual word is wrong.
  - Source: https://anthology.ach.org/volumes/vol0003/how-scalable-is-quality-assessment-of-text-of/10.63744@GR59c1iXu6Wj.pdf
  - Quote: "The regression analyses reveal strong and consistent relationships between WC scores and WER across all three datasets."
  - Relevance: Justifies a page-level triage rule (route low-mean-confidence statement pages to a stronger engine or manual review) while showing the evidence does not extend to per-row or per-amount error detection.
  - Caveats: Bubula et al., Computational Humanities Research 2025 (Berlin State Library), numbers from Table 1. Historical German/Fraktur and Belgian newspapers, not financial documents. The aggregation unit of each data point is not stated explicitly in the text I read (page-level is my inference). Models: german_print (LSTM) for the first two sets, deu_frak for the third; the appendix says one of these is a converted Tesseract 3 model and warns the two are 'not directly comparable in operation, including confidence calculation'. Exact Tesseract 4.x point release not given. No per-word precision/recall or ECE reported.
- **G1-confidence.9** (access: full_text; evidence: measured): I found no published measurement of per-word expected calibration error or confidence-threshold precision/recall for the Tesseract 4/5 LSTM engine. The nearest receipt/form study (CORD, SROIE, FUNSD plus a private set) excludes Tesseract; for the six engines it tested, word-box ECE ranged from 1.1 to 22.6 and a confidence-threshold-only error detector reached F1 between 0.22 and 0.88, with the open-source engines calibrated worse than the commercial ones.

## G2-evaluation

- **G2-evaluation.1** (access: full_text; evidence: measured): Geifman & El-Yaniv (NIPS 2017) define coverage phi(f,g)=E_P[g(x)] and selective risk R(f,g)=E_P[l(f(x),y)g(x)]/phi(f,g) (eq. 1), and give an algorithm (SGR) that picks a confidence threshold on a held-out labelled i.i.d. set S_m so that Pr_{S_m}{R(f,g) > r*} < delta (eq. 2). The bound b* is the exact binomial-tail inversion: solve sum_{j=0}^{m*r_hat} C(m,j) b^j (1-b)^(m-j) = delta (Lemma 3.1, eq. 4), applied with delta/ceil(log2 m) for the binary search (Theorem 3.2). Measured, delta=0.001, about 5000 calibration and 5000 test samples, CIFAR-10 (Table 1): target risk 0.01 gave test risk 0.0092 at test coverage 0.7856, bound 0.0099; target 0.02 gave test risk 0.0149 at coverage 0.8466. INFERENCE (mine): with zero observed errors eq. 4 reduces to (1-b)^m = delta, i.e. b = 1 - delta^(1/m), the same expression as the zero-error Clopper-Pearson bound in finding 4.
  - Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.08500
  - Quote: "an unprecedented 2% error in top-5 ImageNet classification can be guaranteed with probability 99.9%, and almost 60% test coverage."
  - Relevance: Gives the exact definitions (coverage = share of rows auto-accepted, selective risk = error rate among auto-accepted rows) and a procedure with a stated confidence for choosing the auto-accept threshold of a pipeline with human-review fallback.
  - Caveats: Image classification with softmax confidence, 2017; not OCR or document extraction. Guarantee assumes the calibration set is i.i.d. from the deployment distribution; rows from the same statement are not independent (inference). Footnote 2: authors did not apply a Bonferroni correction across the 6-7 target risks per table. Footnote 1: if the confidence score ranks poorly the bound still holds but coverage collapses. The reduction to 1-delta^(1/m) is my own algebra.
- **G2-evaluation.2** (access: full_text; evidence: documentation): Geifman, Uziel & El-Yaniv (ICLR 2019) define the area under the risk-coverage curve as AURC(kappa,f|V_n) = (1/n) * sum over theta in Theta of r_hat(f,g_theta|V_n), where Theta is the set of confidence values of the n labelled points and r_hat is empirical selective risk; lower is better. The best achievable value for a classifier with full-coverage error r_hat is AURC(kappa*) ~= r_hat + (1-r_hat)ln(1-r_hat) (eq. 3 and following), and Excess-AURC = AURC(kappa) - AURC(kappa*), which is 0 for a confidence score that ranks every error below every correct prediction. Worked example in the paper: CIFAR-100 with n=10,000, full-coverage risk about 0.29, risk about 0.06 at coverage 0.5, optimal AURC 0.04802.
  - Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.08206
  - Quote: "We propose to measure the performance of a κ function as the area under the risk-coverage curve (AURC) of a selective classifier induced by κ."
  - Relevance: AURC / E-AURC is the summary number for comparing confidence signals (OCR word confidence, cross-engine agreement, balance checks) independent of a chosen threshold; E-AURC separates quality of the confidence ranking from base accuracy.
  - Caveats: Definitions from image classification, not documents. AURC averages over all coverages, so it weights the high-coverage region the same as the very-low-risk region that matters for exact extraction; reporting coverage at a fixed target risk is not replaced by it (inference). AURC depends on base error r_hat, which is why the paper normalises to E-AURC.
- **G2-evaluation.3** (access: full_text; evidence: documentation): Conformal risk control (Angelopoulos, Bates, Fisch, Lei, Schuster; arXiv 2208.02814) guarantees only the EXPECTED loss on a new exchangeable point, E[L_{n+1}(lambda_hat)] <= alpha (Theorem 1), not a bound holding with a stated confidence. The threshold is lambda_hat = inf{lambda : (n/(n+1)) R_hat_n(lambda) + B/(n+1) <= alpha} (eq. 4), valid only for a loss that is non-increasing in lambda, right-continuous and bounded by B; for non-monotone losses control can fail (Proposition 1). Tightness: E[L] >= alpha - 2B/(n+1) for i.i.d. continuous losses. A guarantee with stated confidence is instead given by Learn-then-Test (arXiv 2110.01052, Definition 1): P(R(T_lambda_hat) <= alpha) >= 1 - delta over the draw of an i.i.d. calibration set, obtained by multiple hypothesis testing with finite-sample p-values; its Section 3.3 covers predictors that abstain.
  - Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.02814
  - Quote: "it has a guarantee in expectation, and neither the algorithm nor its analysis share much technical similarity with these previous works."
  - Relevance: For an undetected-error budget the report must distinguish 'risk <= alpha on average over calibration draws' (CRC) from 'risk <= alpha with probability 1-delta' (SGR, LTT); only the latter matches a statement like 'at most 0.1% wrong rows with 95% confidence'.
  - Caveats: LTT quote read in full text of https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.01052: "We say that Tλ̂ is an (α, δ)-riskcontrolling prediction (RCP) if P(R(Tλ̂ ) ≤ α) ≥ 1 − δ." Both papers' experiments are vision/NLP/tabular, none on OCR. Both assume exchangeable or i.i.d. calibration and deployment data; a new statement layout or scan quality breaks this (inference). The selective error rate among accepted rows is a ratio, not obviously monotone in the threshold, so plain CRC does not directly apply to it (inference; LTT handles non-monotone risks).
- **G2-evaluation.4** (access: full_text; evidence: documentation): With 0 errors in n independent audited samples, the exact one-sided 95% upper confidence bound on the error rate is p_U = 1 - 0.05^(1/n) (Clopper-Pearson, x=0), approximated by 3/n. Hanley & Lippman-Hand (JAMA 1983) derive it from (1 - max risk)^n = 0.05 and tabulate exact vs rule (Table 2): 0/10 -> 26% (30%), 0/20 -> 14% (15%), 0/30 -> 10% (10%), 0/50 -> 6% (6%), 0/100 -> 3% (3%), 0/1000 -> 0.3% (0.3%); the 99% and 99.9% analogues are 4.6/n and 6.9/n. OWN COMPUTATION from the exact formula: 0/300 -> 0.994%; about 2,995 error-free samples are needed to bound the rate below 0.1% and about 29,956 to bound it below 0.01% at 95% confidence.
  - Source: https://jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/Reprints/If_Nothing_Goes_1983.pdf
  - Quote: "if none of n patients shows the event about which we are concerned, we can be 95% confident that the chance of this event is at most three in n (ie, 3/n)."
  - Relevance: Converts a clean audit of auto-accepted transaction rows into a defensible ceiling on the undetected-error rate and shows how large the audit must be: a zero-error audit of a few hundred rows only supports a claim of about 1%.
  - Caveats: Author-hosted scan of the JAMA paper with no text layer; read as page images, table values transcribed by eye. Exact general formula confirmed in F. Scholz, STAT 498B slides, Univ. of Washington 2007 (https://faculty.washington.edu/fscholz/DATAFILES/ConfBoundsSlides.pdf): "p̂U (γ, 0, n) = 1 − (1 − γ)1/n" and upper bound = qbeta(γ, x+1, n−x) for x<n; Scholz says the 3/n step is valid for large n, 'say n ≥ 100', Hanley says it agrees to the nearest percentage point above n=30. Assumes independent random samples; rows within one statement share scan defects, so the effective n is smaller than the row count (inference, consistent with ISRI treating pages, not characters, as independent). The bound applies only to the population the audit was sampled from.
- **G2-evaluation.5** (access: full_text; evidence: documentation): Key-information-extraction benchmarks score a field as all-or-nothing on content plus category, but differ on whether text is checked at all. SROIE Task 3 (ICDAR 2019): a field is correct only if content and category both match ground truth, ranked by F1 over all test receipts; 600 trainval / 400 test receipts, the test images released only 'a few weeks before the submission deadline'; best Task 3 score 90.49%, more than half of submissions below 80%. Donut (ECCV 2022, on CORD): field-level F1 fails a field on one missing character and cannot measure grouping structure, so a tree-edit-distance accuracy max(0, 1 - TED(pr,gt)/TED(empty,gt)) is added. DocILE (ICDAR 2023) line-item recognition: micro-F1 over line-item fields where a field is correct on field type, location and line-item assignment (maximum matching of predicted to ground-truth line items); the primary metric deliberately ignores the recognised text.
  - Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.10213
  - Quote: "An extract text is marked as correct if both submitted content and category of the extracted text matches the groundtruth; Otherwise, marked as incorrect."
  - Relevance: Supplies precedent for the report's metric: field-level exact match on (value, field type) plus a row/record-level criterion that also checks assignment of fields to the right row; and warns that a leaderboard F1 such as DocILE's says nothing about exactness of amounts.
  - Caveats: Donut quote (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.15664, Sec. 3.2): "Even if a single character is missed, the score assumes the field extraction is failed." DocILE quote (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.05658, Sec. 4.1): "we choose an evaluation metric (below) that does not pay attention to the text recognition part"; DocILE baseline LIR F1 0.686-0.721 (Table 3). SROIE paper says 'mAP' and F1 loosely and does not spell out string normalisation; the scoring script itself was not read. None of these benchmarks defines an all-fields-correct-per-row or per-document exact-match rate; that is my proposed extension (inference). Receipts and invoices, not card statements. CORD's own paper was not read.
- **G2-evaluation.6** (access: full_text; evidence: documentation): ANLS was designed to give partial credit to answers with OCR character errors, so it cannot serve as an exactness metric. Definition (Biten et al., ICCV 2019, eq. 1): ANLS = (1/N) sum_i max_j s(a_ij, o_qi), with s = 1 - NL if NL < tau and 0 otherwise, NL the normalized Levenshtein distance, tau = 0.5. Measured examples from the ANLS* paper (arXiv 2402.03848, Tables 1-2): a transposition typo 'Hello World' vs 'Hello Wolrd' scores 0.82; '31.12.2023' vs '31.Dec 2023' scores 0.58; 0.2 vs 0.199999999 scores 0.0 because numbers are compared as strings; a list with one of two elements missing scores 0.5. INFERENCE (mine): a single wrong digit in an 8-character amount gives NL = 1/8 and a score of 0.875, so a set of amounts with one wrong digit in each would still report ANLS near 0.88.
  - Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.13648
  - Quote: "the metric we propose named Average Normalized Levenshtein Similarity (ANLS) would give an intermediate score between 0.5 and 1 that will softly penalise the OCR mistakes."
  - Relevance: Direct textual evidence that string-similarity metrics hide exactness errors by design: a wrong amount or date is rewarded almost as much as a right one, and semantically equal values in another format are punished. Supports using typed, normalised exact match (decimal amount, ISO date, currency code) as the primary metric.
  - Caveats: ANLS was built for scene-text VQA, where soft credit is the stated goal; the papers do not themselves call this a flaw for financial data, that reading is mine. ANLS* quote (Sec. 4.1): "numbers are not interpreted as numbers, but a string comparison is performed instead." The 0.875 digit example is my own arithmetic, not from either paper.
- **G2-evaluation.7** (access: full_text; evidence: measured): On table extraction, partial-credit cell-similarity scores stay near 1 while exact-match table accuracy is far lower. PubTables-1M (Smock et al., CVPR 2022, Table 4, canonical test data): DETR reaches GriTS_Con 0.9846 and adjacency F-score 0.9774 but Acc_Con (all cells' text exactly right) of 0.8138 overall and 0.6944 on complex tables; Faster R-CNN reaches GriTS_Con 0.8538 with Acc_Con 0.1039. GriTS_Con is defined as 2*sum_{i,j} f(A~_ij, B~_ij)/(|A|+|B|) over the 2D most-similar substructures of the ground-truth and predicted cell matrices, with f = normalized longest common subsequence of cell text, so a cell with one wrong character still earns most of its credit.
  - Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.00061
  - Quote: "we report the table content accuracy metric (AccCont ), which is the percentage of tables whose text content matches the ground truth exactly for every cell"
  - Relevance: Measured gap of 17 to 75 points between a similarity score and exact match on the same outputs; the report should lead with exact-match rates at cell, row and table (statement) level and treat GriTS/TEDS-style numbers as diagnostics only.
  - Caveats: Born-digital scientific tables from PubMed Central with text taken from the PDF, not OCR of degraded scans; the gap there is driven by structure errors, and OCR errors would add to it (inference). GriTS paper (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.12555, Sec. 1) on the exact-match measure: "historically this metric has been eschewed in favor of measures of partial correctness", partly because partial measures 'are less impacted by errors and ambiguities in the ground truth'. Neither paper defines a row-level exact-match rate. TEDS was not read.
- **G2-evaluation.8** (access: full_text; evidence: measured): Train/test template overlap inflates document-extraction scores by several F1 points. Laatiri et al. (BNP Paribas, arXiv 2304.14936, 2023) found 75% template replication in SROIE's official test set and that 8 of 50 FUNSD test forms (16%) share a template with a training form. After re-splitting so that no template (same merchant/layout) appears in both splits, average F1 on SROIE fell from 94.34 to 85.60 and the best model from 96.55 to 89.38; text-only models lost 10.5 points on average, multimodal ones 7.5. The authors call this a data leak in the original splits. Protocols that avoid it: DocILE keeps test annotations private with server-side scoring and builds the test set from 25% zero-shot, 25% few-shot and 50% many-shot layouts; Seuret et al. (arXiv 2305.07131) split OCR training data at book level.
  - Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.14936
  - Quote: "the average F1 score drops from 94.34 to 85.60 and the highest reached F1 drops from 96.55 to 89.38."
  - Relevance: A positional parser and OCR settings tuned on the same statements (or the same issuer layout and scan batch) used for evaluation will overstate accuracy; the report should require splitting by statement and by issuer/layout/scan batch, and a final test set touched once.
  - Caveats: Evidence is for learned KIE models (BERT, LayoutLM family) on receipts and forms, not for hand-tuned OCR preprocessing or rule parsers; transfer to tuning Tesseract parameters on evaluation scans is my inference, no measured study of that was found. Arxiv preprint from an industry lab; abstract says 'on average 10,5% F1' while the body says F1 points. DocILE quote (arXiv 2302.05658, Sec. 3.4): "The test set annotations are not public". Seuret quote (Sec. 4): "Our data is split at the book level so that no data from the same book can be present in both training and test sets."
- **G2-evaluation.9** (access: full_text; evidence: measured): The ISRI/UNLV Fourth Annual Test of OCR Accuracy (Rice, Jenkins, Nartker, 1995) evaluated 8 engines on randomly selected pages (over three million characters, five samples) run fully automatically with no human interaction, defined character accuracy = (#characters - #errors)/#characters with errors = minimum edit operations, and computed approximate 95% confidence intervals by jackknife over pages, assuming pages but not characters are independent. Its 'marked character efficiency' test measured the engines' own reject/suspect flags as a review aid: examining the flagged 0.5% of output characters let an editor correct only 20 to 45% of the errors, with efficiency falling as more low-confidence marks were examined. Stated differently (my restatement): at that review budget 55 to 80% of character errors were unflagged.
  - Source: https://tesseract-ocr.github.io/docs/AT-1995.pdf
  - Quote: "Marked characters make it possible for an editor to inspect only one-half of one percent of the OCR-generated text yet correct 20 to 45% of the errors in the text."
  - Relevance: Earliest large measured risk-coverage style result for OCR confidence as a human-review trigger, and a protocol template: random sampling of test pages, automatic scoring, document-level (clustered) confidence intervals. It shows engine confidence alone leaves most errors undetected at small review budgets.
  - Caveats: 1994-era commercial engines (Caere, XIS, HP Labs, Recognita and others) on letters, DOE reports, magazines and newspapers; says nothing about Tesseract 4/5 LSTM confidences. Character-level, not field-level. The report does not say whether test pages were withheld from vendors, and the Magazine sample was re-used from the third test as a year-over-year yardstick, so it is not evidence about leakage. Confidence-interval quote (Sec. 2.2): "we have made the assumption that the pages within a sample are independent, but we have not assumed that the characters within a page are independent."
